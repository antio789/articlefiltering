TITLE: Effect of hydraulic retention time on deterioration/restarting of sludge anaerobic digestion: Extracellular polymeric substances and microbial response


1 Introduction

Activated sludge process is indisputably the most frequently employed technique in municipal wastewater treatment plants (Jang et al., 2014). However, this process produces significant amounts of waste sludge, which poses serious environmental problems (Gryta et al., 2014; Mills et al., 2014). On the other hand, owing to its nutrient properties, resource utilization of waste sludge has also attracted much interest in recent years. Considering the advantages of volume reducing, biomass stabilization and energy recovery, anaerobic digestion has been widely applied for sludge treatment (Lin et al., 2009; Yang et al., 2015). Typically, the anaerobic digestion process degrades about 20–40% of the total chemical oxygen demand (COD) and produces approximately 140–180 mL CH_4/gCOD during steady-state operation (Jang et al., 2014).

Compared to aerobic biodegradation, anaerobic digestion is restricted by operational factors such as temperature, hydraulic retention time (HRT) and the occurrence of inhibitors (e.g., resin acids, sulfur, and organochlorine, etc.). The above mentioned, may negatively affect the performance of digesters, and even lead to the total collapse of the anaerobic system (Chen et al., 2014; Meyer and Edwards, 2014). For example, among the 50 anaerobic digesters constructed in China over recent decades, more than 60% are poorly operated, and some systems have even failed. Thus, it is necessary to determine the mechanisms by which anaerobic digestion deteriorates, as well as a process for restarting such deteriorated systems. Franke-Whittle et al. (2014) revealed that organic overloading is one of the most common reasons for deterioration of digesters, although traditionally operating an anaerobic digester at low HRT (10–15 d) is welcome in practice. Correspondingly, the ethanol/volatile fatty acids (VFAs) accumulation and low COD removal rate caused by the imbalance in microbial activities, is a negative effect of the system deterioration, leading to its eventual collapse (Krakat et al., 2010; Dennehy et al., 2017).

Parameters such as COD, pH and biogas production rates, well-known standard analyses for anaerobic digestion, have been widely used to assess digester performance. However, these parameters are always subject to time-lapsed response and exhibit significant daily variation. Thus, other parameters, including the concentration of extracellular polymeric substances (EPS) and their fractional variations have recently been used as novel approaches for facilitating and interpreting the removal of organics during sludge digestion. Generally, EPS account for 50–90% of the total organic carbon in sludge organics and are hydrolyzed to serve as carbon and energy sources for gas production during anaerobic digestion (Kavitha et al., 2014). Such high molecular-weight polymers exhibit a complex structure and could not be easily destructed. To clarify the environmental behaviors of those insoluble sludge EPS (sEPS), ammonium hydroxide has been used as an effective extraction reagent because of its negligible influence on the chemical characteristics of the extracts (Jiang et al., 2010). Besides, the effect of sEPS components (hydrophobic/hydrophilic materials, humic acid, proteins and polysaccharides) on the performance of digesters was rigorously investigated, using ammonium hydroxide extraction combined with XAD fractionation technology. The rate-limiting factor of anaerobic digestion under steady-state operation was thus obtained (Wei et al., 2011, 2012). However, limited information is available about the transformation of sludge organics during the deterioration/restarting of digestion processes.

For the work reported in this paper, an anaerobic digester was operated from steady-state to collapse, and vice versa, by gradually changing the HRT; thus the fractional variation and biodegradation behavior of sEPS were empirically evaluated. Sequenced genomes from the biomass within the reactors were periodically analyzed to compare different system conditions with the steady-state one. Furthermore, the hydrolysis/degradation of the sEPS during collapse condition was assessed.


2 Materials and methods


2.1 Sludge characterization

Sludge (feed) was collected from the secondary sedimentation tank in a municipal wastewater treatment plant of Harbin (China), and then stored at 4 °C. The sludge had a pH of 6.9, volatile solids (VS) content of 16,200–19,500 mg/L, total solids (TS) content of 16,800–20,300 mg/L, COD of 24,500–29,200 mg/L and soluble chemical oxygen demand (SCOD) of 1000–1350 mg/L.


2.2 Anaerobic digestion test

Each of the lab-scale digesters, with adjacent gas storage tanks, consisted of a 25 cm diameter Plexiglas cylinder with a working volume of 5 L. The schematic diagram of the digester is given in Fig. S1 in the Supplementary Material provided with this paper. The cover of the digester incorporated three separate ports for different functions: feeding, mechanical agitation (stirred at 80 r/min), and measurement of biogas generation (using a gas counter). The feed sludge, which was stored at 4 °C to prevent hydrolysis of organics during storage, was pumped to each reactor using peristaltic pumps. To maintain the high sludge retention time required for sludge digestion (10–30 d), the peristaltic pumps were programmed to operate three times per day; at these times, corresponding volume of digested sludge was pumped out simultaneously.

The digester was first acclimated for 2 months (using digested sludge as inoculum) at 30 d HRT and 35 ± 1 °C to reach steady methanogenesis. The volume of inoculum used was 0.5 L, which equals to 10% of the working volume of the experimental digester. Then, the HRT was reduced stepwise to 20 d (for 1 month), 15 d (for 1 month) and 10 d (for 2 months). Subsequently, the HRT was prolonged to 20 d for recovery (2 months). Biogas production (quantity and composition) and the main parameters such as pH, alkalinity, TS, SCOD and VFAs content, were daily measured.


2.3 EPS extraction and fractionation

EPS samples were extracted from the experimental sludge following the procedure of Chen et al. (2003). For fractionation, the water content of the sludge was adjusted to 97.0%, after which it was centrifuged for 30 min (4000g). Then, 100g of the solid residue collected after centrifugation was slowly mixed with 200 mL of 25% (v/v) NH_4OH for 24 h, and passed through a 0.45 μm membrane filter. The filtrate containing sEPS so collected was diluted (50 times) and acidified to pH 2. Then, the supernatant organics and sEPS were sorted into the following five fractions using XAD-8/XAD-4 resins: hydrophobic acid (HPO-A), hydrophobic neutral (HPO-N), transphilic acid (TPI-A), transphilic neutral (TPI-N), and hydrophilic (HPI).

Briefly, 5 L of acidified organic solutions (filtered by 0.45 μm filters) were passed through the XAD-8 and -4 resin columns at a flow rate of 10 bed volumes (BV) per hour. The HPI fraction was composed of the organics in the XAD-4 effluent. Each of the resin columns was backward eluted separately with 0.1 mol/L NaOH at a flow rate of 2 BV/h, followed by 2 BV of Milli-Q water. The eluate from XAD-8 was defined as HPO-A, and that from XAD-4, as TPI-A. HPO-N and TPI-N fractions were those organics adsorbed onto XAD-8 and -4 resins, respectively, but which did not dissolve during the back elution. A complete description of the fractionation method can be found elsewhere (Xue et al., 2008).


2.4 DNA extraction, cloning and sequencing

Biomass samples collected from steady-state and collapsed/recovered reactors were saved for DNA extraction. First, the biomass samples were filtered through 0.22 μm membrane filters (Millipore Laboratories, Billerica, MA) and then stored at −80 °C. DNA in these samples was extracted using UltraClean^® Soil DNA Isolation Kits (MoBIO Laboratories, Carlsbad, CA) and subsequently purified via ethanol precipitation. Extracted DNA was quantified using a NanoDrop spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA). To detect the archaeal community within the digester, gDNA was amplified with primers specific to the mcrA gene according to the polymerase chain reaction (PCR) protocols (Luton et al., 2002). To enable sample multiplexing during sequencing, barcodes were incorporated between the adapter and forward primer. Triplicate PCRs were performed for each sample, and then the results of 454 pyrosequencing were analyzed. A detailed description of the procedures for bacterial community-related DNA extraction and amplification can be found in Fitzgerald et al. (2015).


2.5 Analytical methods


2.5.1 Chemical analysis

All the sEPS and supernatant samples were diluted (50 and 15 times for sEPS and supernatant, respectively), filtered through 0.45 μm cellulose nitrate membranes, and then stored at 4 °C. All measurements were run in triplicate. The dissolved organic carbon (DOC) in the filtrate was analyzed using a TOC-5000 total organic carbon analyzer (Shimadzu, Kyoto, Japan). The sludge COD, SCOD, VS and TS content were measured according to Standard Methods (APHA, 2002). The protein contents in the extracted sEPS and supernatant organics were determined using the modified Lowry method (Frølund et al., 1996) with bovine serum albumin as standard. The carbohydrate content was measured using the Anthrone method with glucose as standard. VFAs were quantified with a gas chromatograph (Agilent 6890, USA), using a flame ionization detector (FID) equipped with a Nukol capillary column (30 m × 0.25mm × 0.25 μm). The carrier gas was helium. The injector and detector temperatures were 220 and 250 °C, respectively, and the oven temperature was set at 150 °C for 3 min and thereafter increased to 180 °C. The detection limit for VFAs analysis was 5.0 mg/L. Primary standard solutions of the VFAs were obtained from Aldrich Chemical Company (Milwaukee, Wisconsin, USA).


2.5.2 Fluorescence spectroscopy

Excitation-emission matrix (EEM) was measured in a 1 cm cuvette using a Jasco FP-6500 spectrofluorometer (Tokyo, Japan) at 24 °C. The organic samples were diluted to 1 mg DOC/L using 0.01 mol/L KCl and acidified to pH 3 with HCl. A xenon lamp was the excitation source, with the excitation and emission slits set to a 5 nm band-pass. Each EEM lot was generated by scanning excitation wavelengths from 220 to 400 nm with 5 nm steps and emitting fluorescence between 280 and 480 nm with 1 nm steps.


3 Results and discussion


3.1 Organics removal, VFA accumulation, and digester performance

Anaerobic sludge digester was operated at HRTs of 20, 15, 10, and then to 20 d to assess the rate and extent of methanogenesis under different operational conditions. Fig. 1 presents the experimental results, including biogas yields, TCOD removal rates, and variations of pH and alkalinity during the whole operational process. Production rates of biogas increased from 305.2 to 543.8 mL/d during the first 11 days of operation in 20 d HRT condition. Thereafter, the rates fluctuated and were stabilized at mean values of 560.5 ± 87 mL/d. Switching the HRT to 15 d (with a higher organic loading of 1.77 kg TCOD/m^3 d) led to increased biogas production, which then stabilized at 753 ± 52 mL/d for biogas and 509.8 ± 41.1 mL/d for CH_4, respectively. Besides the increase in biogas volume, a significant decrease in TCOD removal efficiency (from 43.4 ± 2.7% to 33.6 ± 2.5%) was observed (Fig. 1). Further shortening of the HRT to 10 d destabilized the anaerobic process, with a decrease in biogas production. Meanwhile, a significant accumulation of SCOD (2.85 times than at 20 d HRT) was observed within the supernatant of the digesters, indicating inhibition of the methanogenic biomass at the lower HRT condition. Once the operational HRT was prolonged to 20 d, biogas production increased gradually within 60 d. This clearly implied that the deteriorated digesters could benefit from the HRT prolonging.

As shown in Fig. 2 , high concentration of total VFAs in the supernatant of sludge digester, during the operation at 10 d HRT (days 90–120), was mainly related to the acetic acid accumulation (740 mg/L at day 120 of operation). Comparatively, the concentration of the accumulated propionic, butyric and caproic acid was 370 mg/L, 240 mg/L and 170 mg/L, respectively. For comparison, the relatively lower VFAs concentration observed at 20 and 15 d HRT (<200 mg/L) implied more stable operation of the digester under such condition (Wang et al., 2014). Although, the biogas yield decreased slightly during the first 30 d of the operation at the 10 d HRT, sharp declines in alkalinity and biogas production were observed when the operation period was prolonged to 2 months. There was as simultaneous significant increase in VFAs production (about 15 times), demonstrating that the anaerobic digestion was close to collapse. These results are consistent with the observations of Salminen and Rintala (2002), who found a gradual accumulation of total VFAs, and a simultaneous decline in methane yield for anaerobic digestion of poultry slaughterhouse wastes when the HRT declined from 25 to 13 d.


3.2 Transformation of sludge organics during the deterioration/restarting process


3.2.1 Transformation of sludge DOC

The HPI and HPO-A fractions were the principal components in the sEPS (113.5 mg/g VS and 116.8 mg/g VS, respectively), representing 37.9% and 36.8% of the bulk DOC (Fig. 3 ). The remaining organic fractions of the raw sEPS were TPI-A (12.5%), HPO-N (7.4%), and TPI-N (5.3%). HPI was the predominant component in the sludge supernatant (accounting for 42.3% of the bulk DOC), followed by HPO-A (33.0%); whereas the fractions of the TPI-A (9.4%), HPO-N (3.4%), and TPI-N (2.3%) were quite low. The main objective of this study was to evaluate the transformation of sludge organics under the deterioration and restarting processes in an anaerobic digester. To this end, the fractional DOC distribution, and removal trend of sEPS and supernatants during days 60–180 of operation were emphatically analyzed and discussed here. The conditions included HRT from 15 to 10 d (for deterioration), and 10–20 d (for restarting).

The digester showed steady and continuous biogas production from the waste activated sludge at each HRT conditions of 30 d (acclimation period), 20 d and 15 d. The corresponding DOC removal rate was 58.6 ± 3.6%, 37.1 ± 2.9%, and 30.2 ± 2.8%, respectively. In contrast, long-term operation of the anaerobic digester under 10 d HRT led to the decline of sludge hydrolysis and eventual collapse of the process. As shown in Fig. 3, the removal rate of sEPS-related DOC at the 10 d HRT was 25.6% after 30 d of digestion, and then gradually declined to 22.8%, 18.1%, and 10.9% once the operational period was prolonged to 40, 50 and 60 days, respectively. The low hydrolysis rate of sEPS and significant accumulation of VFAs (about 15 times that observed in Fig. 2) implied that the digestion process was starting to deteriorate, similar to the observations made by Dong et al. (2011). In terms of sEPS fractions, HPO-A exhibited a relatively low DOC removal rate (<5.0%) during long-term operation at 10 d HRT (i.e. after the digestion had deteriorated); thus, its accumulation in the digester demonstrated that it is not efficiently hydrolyzed under such conditions. TPI-A exhibited the next lowest DOC removal rate (9.2%). In contrast, the fractions of HPI (10.6%), TPI-N (21.7%) and HPO-N (23.2%), showed that they could be partially biodegraded. Their related supernatant organics increased 203–289% during the deterioration and were accumulated within the digester. Thus, it can be concluded that hydrolysis/accumulation, as well as insignificant acidogenesis of those four fractions, might be other reasons for VFAs accumulation. Those results are partially consistent with the observations of Bernal-Martinez et al. (2009) and Wei et al. (2011).

To restart the anaerobic system from a state of deterioration, an operational HRT of 20 d was chosen for enhancing the methanogenesis (after 60 days operation at 10 d HRT). Overall, the decline in organic loading gradually restricted the accumulation of VFAs (Fig. 2), and enhanced the biodegradation of the sEPS (Fig. 3). Fifteen days after restarting the process (at 20 d HRT), the accumulated HPO-A and VFAs were partially degraded. For example, the concentration of acetic acid, propionic acid, butyric acid and caproic acid in the sludge supernatant decreased from 741.3, 370.6, 240.3 and 170.9 mg/L to 523.4, 337.3, 188.3 and 141.6 mg/L, respectively (Fig. 2). Moreover, the DOC removal rate of all five sEPS fractions exhibited an increase compared with the deteriorated condition, the lowest value recorded for HPO-A (3.6%), and ranging from 23.9 to 38.7% for the other four fractions (HPI, 23.9% vs HPO-N, 38.7%). Despite the fact that they continued to remain lower than before deterioration, the removal rates of these five sEPS fractions exhibited a significant increase when the restarting period was prolonged to 30 d. Thereafter, the removal rates of the predominant fractions of HPO-A and HPI was 22.1% and 25.4%, respectively. After 60 days of recovery at 20 d HRT, the removal rate of the unfractionated sEPS (35.1%) was close to that before collapse, for HPI was slightly higher and for HPO-A was lower. This can be attributed to changes in the dominance and growth of the biomass during the whole operational period. Fig. S2 in the Supplementary Material revealed that the hydrolyzed sEPS accumulated within the supernatant after long-term operation at 10 d HRT, especially for the easily biodegradable fraction of HPI. This implied that the methanogenesis was rate-limiting for the anaerobic digestion process under low HRT.


3.2.2 Transformation of sludge carbohydrates and proteins

Fractional distribution of proteins and carbohydrates in the feed and digested sEPS are separately shown in Fig. 4 (a) and (b). In terms of quantity, proteins were predominant in the sEPS regardless of the condition of the digestion process (Fig. 4a). It was 114.3 mg/g VS for the raw sEPS, and 70.5 and 79.4 mg/g VS for the digested sEPS under 20 d HRT and 15 d HRT, respectively. As shown in Fig. 4b, when HRT declined from 30 to 20 to 15 d, the concentration of the carbohydrates in the digested sEPS gradually increased from 20.5 to 32.3 to 35.5 mg/g VS (average 64.2 mg/g VS for the feed). Long-term operation of the digester under 10 d HRT negatively affected the hydrolyzation of carbohydrates, and the hydrolysis rate obtained for carbohydrates was 35.6%, 22.2% and 13.7% after 30, 50, and 60 d of digestion, respectively. Undoubtedly, prolonging the HRT to 20 d gradually improved the performance of the digestion and enhanced hydrolysis, achieving a 40.1% removal efficiency of carbohydrates after 60 d restarting, which is close to the level before reactor deterioration. Similarly, the hydrolysis rate of proteins-related sEPS declined significantly when the operational HRT decreased, with average removal rates of 58.8%, 38.3% and 30.5% observed for the 30 d, 20 d, and 15 d HRT digestion, respectively. Further shortening of the HRT to 10 d contributed to deterioration of protein hydrolysis, as proven by the lower removal rate of 21.7%, 16.7%, and 11.5% for protein after the 30, 45 and 60 d of operation, respectively. Such lower removal efficiency clearly demonstrated that sEPS-related protein had not been efficiently hydrolyzed, especially after the 60 d acidification/deterioration. Generally, sEPS-related proteins exhibited a relatively lower hydrolysis rate than that of carbohydrates, which is consistent with the observation of Bougrier et al. (2008) and Ahring et al. (2015), who found that carbohydrates in sludge biomass were more easily hydrolyzed or degraded than proteins during anaerobic digestion. Restarting the digester at 20 d HRT efficiently enhanced the hydrolysis of proteins within the sEPS and finally increased the protein removal rate to 35.7% after two months of recovery.

Figs. S3 and S4 in the Supplementary Material shows that the concentration of both carbohydrates and proteins in the sludge supernatant increased significantly once the digestion deteriorated. For example, carbohydrates content in the sludge supernatant increased 85.2% and 134.5% after 45 and 60 days of continuous operation, respectively, at the 10 d HRT; and proteins content increased 103.4% and 212.1%, respectively, instead. The above results demonstrated that biodegradation of the hydrolyzed sEPS, especially the protein-related sEPS, was the rate-limiting factor for the anaerobic digester at 10 d HRT, similar to the observations of Rani et al. (2014) and Zhen et al. (2014). Once the HRT was prolonged to 20 d, the accumulated carbohydrates and proteins (high-molecular-weight heterogeneous polymers) could be efficiently removed after 30 d of recovery.

The carbohydrates and proteins of the raw sEPS were mainly in the form of HPO-A, accounting for 46.2% of the carbohydrates and 60.3% of the bulk proteins, respectively. The contents in HPI, HPO-N, TPI-A and TPI-N were comparably lower. For proteins, the distributions of the other four fractions followed the trend HPI (17.1%) > TPI-A (11.0%) > HPO-N (6.8%) > TPI-N (4.8%), while the carbohydrates decreased in the order of HPI (28.6%) > TPI-A (14.4%) > HPO-N (5.9%) > TPI-N (4.9%). Generally, the removal efficiency of the hydrophobic proteins in sEPS was much lower than that of the hydrophilic ones under each HRT. For example, the bulk removal rate of HPO-A related proteins at the 20 d and 15 d HRT was 34.7% and 26.1%, respectively. This is much lower than the corresponding values for the hydrophilic proteins (86.0% and 62.0%), demonstrating that those HPI-related proteins could easily be hydrolyzed and further biodegraded. Li et al. (2016) pointed out that the surface of anaerobic granular sludge was more hydrophilic than the interior; meanwhile carbohydrates were responsible for hydrophilicity, and proteins for hydrophobicity. This might be the main reason why proteins exhibited a lower hydrolysis rate regardless of HRT variation. Similarly, Mahoney et al. (1987) reported that the proteins within the non-granular sludge washed out from the upflow anaerobic sludge blanket were more hydrophilic than the reactor sludge, supporting the hypothesis that these hydrophilic substances were easily hydrolyzed. When the HRT decreased to 10 d, the removal rate of the hydrophobic proteins declined significantly; the hydrolysis rate of HPO-A related proteins was 21.0% after the 30 d operation and declined further to 11.3% after 60 days of digestion. Simultaneously, the HPO-N declined from 19.9% to 2.3%. By contrast, the removal rate of HPI related proteins at 10 d HRT after 30 and 60 d of digestion was 41.2% and 16.2%, respectively. These results help us conclude that hydrolysis of both the hydrophobic and hydrophilic proteins was severely affected by long-term digestion at 10 d HRT, in which the fractions of HPO-A and HPO-N was seriously affected. Once the HRT was prolonged to 20 d, the removal rate of proteins within each EPS fraction increased significantly. In brief, the removal efficiency of the proteins within the unfractionated sEPS, HPO-A, TPI-A, HPO-N, and TPI-N was close to that before collapse after 30 d of digestion, while that of HPI-related proteins was still lower (i.e., 69.5% after restarting and 86.0% before collapse, on a VS basis). The main reason might be that those accumulated hydrophobic and transphilic proteins within the sludge supernatant would further convert to small molecules (hydrophilic related) (Jiang et al., 2010), leading to a decline in proteins hydrolysis.

The removal trend of carbohydrates in the sEPS was similar to that of the proteins. Specifically, the digestion collapse seriously affected the hydrolysis of HPO-A related carbohydrates but was insignificant for hydrophilic hydrocarbons. As shown in Fig. 4b, the removal rate of HPO-A-related carbohydrates in sEPS decreased to 6.8% after 60 d of digestion under 10 d HRT. Correspondingly, the hydrophilic carbohydrates exhibited a relatively higher removal rate of 35.3%. Thus, hydrolysis of HPI-related carbohydrates provided the main carbon source when the digestion was close to collapse. Sixty days after restarting at 20 d HRT, the hydrolysis rate of hydrophilic carbohydrates reached the same level as before deterioration (20 d HRT). However, the hydrolysis rate of HPO-A related carbohydrates was much lower (36.2% vs 56.7%), which was attributed to the polymeric structure of the carbohydrates in hydrophobic sEPS (Deng et al., 2016).


3.3 Structure changes in sludge organics during deterioration/restarting

Fig. S5 in the Supplementary Material depicts the EEM spectra of the sEPS fractions of the feed, digested sludge from steady-state, deteriorated and recovered digester. There were five principal EEM peaks for the sludge organics before and after digestion, correlating with the regions I-V proposed by Chen et al. (2003). The fluorophores at excitation/emission wavelengths (Ex/Em) of 220–230/290–320 nm and 220–240/320–360 nm (Regions I and II) refer to tyrosine- and tryptophan-related aromatic proteins, respectively. The third peak was at Ex/Em = 230–250/380–430 nm, corresponding to fulvic acid-like components. The fluorescent region at Ex/Em = 260–290/320–370 nm, corresponded to soluble microbial byproduct-like (SMP-like) materials, and the fingerprint with long excitation wavelengths (>265 nm) and emission wavelengths (>380 nm) was related to humic acid-like organics.

The five fractions extracted from the raw sEPS revealed noticeable fluorescence peaks in Regions II–IV, referring to the redundancy of aromatic proteins, fulvic acid-like components, and SMP-like materials. For comparison, the fluorescence peaks related to humic acid-like substances were more like a shoulder. All five sEPS fractions exhibited relatively higher fluorescent intensity in Region III, especially for HPO-A and TPI-A, demonstrating that those fulvic acid-like materials were the main components in those two fractions. Moreover, the fluorophores of each sEPS fraction also showed a higher fluorescent intensity in Region II and IV, clearly revealing that the related proteins and SMP-like substances were abundant existed. In brief, the fluorophore intensity of Region II decreased in the order HPO-A > TPI-A > HPO-N > TPI-N > HPI, while that of Region IV did so as HPO-N > HPO-A > TPI-N > TPI-A > HPI. After long-term operation at 20 d HRT, the fluorescent intensity of Region III declined significantly for each sEPS fraction, demonstrating that fulvic acid-like components were efficiently hydrolyzed when the digestion system was stable. Correspondingly, the fluorescent intensity of the fulvic acid-like fluorophores exhibited a significant and simultaneous increase in the supernatant-related HPO-A and TPI-A. This was related to the accumulation of those hydrolyzed fulvic-like components from sEPS (data not shown). In contrast, the intensity of the proteins related fluorophores and SMP-like components decreased insignificantly, while the fluorophore intensity of the SMP-like materials within TPI-N and HPI increased slightly. This was related to the formation of new soluble aromatic proteins via the growth of biomass in the digesters. Moreover, the major fractions of HPO-A and HPI in the sludge supernatant exhibited an efficient decrease in SMP-like fluorophores, implying that these hydrolyzed SMP-like materials could easily be converted to CH_4.

After 60 d of operations at 10 d HRT, the distribution of the fluorescent intensities demonstrated that the hydrolysis rates of humic acid-, protein- and SMP-like components declined significantly, while that of fulvic acid-like fluorophores was not influenced. Generally, a higher hydrolysis rate of fulvic acid-like components is related to the acidic condition of the digesters caused by VFAs accumulation, as well as to higher solubility of fulvic acid under acidic conditions (Jones et al., 2015). For comparison, the reduced hydrolysis rate of humic acid-like materials was consistent with the observation that the humic acid in sEPS exhibited a refractory characteristic and could not easily be hydrolyzed (Wei et al., 2012). The fluorescent intensity decreased for all five regions (except the fulvic acid-like components) after 60 d of operation at 20 d HRT, clearly demonstrating that the digester was recovering from the VFAs accumulation.


3.4 Microbial responses during deterioration/restarting


3.4.1 Bacterial community composition

The bacterial diversity in the digesters for different operational conditions was investigated by amplicon sequencing of partial 16S rRNA genes. In the anaerobic digester, a diverse biofilm community enhanced the stability of major processes (hydrolysis, acidification and methanogenesis) (Daims and Wagner, 2010). In total, 3769 operational taxonomic units (OTUs) of bacteria were obtained in 20 d HRT digester after 30 d of steady-state operation. This number declined to 2357 after 2 months of cultivation at 10 d HRT (collapsed stage). Fig. 5 a shows the average relative abundance (>1%) of the bacterial genera in at least one sample. Between 6.7% and 11.2% of the reads were not assigned to any known phylum (defined as unclassified bacteria). As shown in Fig. 5a, Firmicutes and Bacteroidetes were the most abundant phyla in the digesters regardless of HRT variations. These two phyla accounted for as much as 23.6% and 21.0% of the bacteria under long-term digestion at 20 d HRT. Moreover, Actinobacteria and Proteobacteria were two other major phyla in 20 d HRT digester, accounting for 5.6% and 12.9% of the bulk bacterial OTUs. These results are in agreement with observations of anaerobic sludge digesters operated under different conditions (Rivière et al., 2009). Other phyla, such as Chloroflexi, Fibrobacteres, Verrucomicrobia, Spirochaetes, Lentisphaerae, and Synergistetes were present in minor proportions (<3.7%).

After about 1 month of operation at 15 d HRT and another 2 months of cultivation at 10 d HRT, the predominant taxa of the biomass changed significantly. In brief, the percentage of Firmicutes increased from 23.6% to 29.9% after deterioration of the digestion, and the Chloroflexi increased from 2.29% to 2.94%. Similarly, the Bacteroidetes exhibited a slight increase from 21.0% to 23.4%. For comparison, the percentage of the Proteobacteria and Actinobacteria decreased from 12.9% and 5.6% to 8.3% and 1.4%, respectively. Ziganshin et al. (2013) demonstrated that the biomass of Firmicutes would convert refractory cellulose into soluble acidic molecules (such as butyrate, propionate and acetate) during anaerobic digestion; thus, the percentage increase in Firmicutes might be the main reason for VFAs accumulation when the digestion deteriorated. Once the digestion process was successfully recovered, the percentage of Firmicutes declined to 24.5%, close to that before collapse. Rivière et al. (2009) pointed out that members of Bacteroidetes are able to degrade proteins and ferment amino acids into acetate. The insignificant variation of Bacteroidetes before and after deterioration (21.0% vs 23.4%) is ascribed to the refractory characteristics of the sEPS proteins. For comparison, Proteocbacteria, members of which are consumers of propionate, butyrate and acetate (Ariesyady et al., 2007), decreased from 12.9% to 8.33% after long-term operation at 10 d HRT, whereas they increased to 13.9% after 60 d of recovery. This was the main reason for the decline in VFAs accumulation. Actinobacteria, which played an important role in cellulose decomposition and hydrophobic humic acids hydrolysis/biodegradation (Petković et al. 2006), decreased sharply after the deterioration at 10 d HRT, but increased when the HRT was prolonged to 20 d.


3.4.2 Archaeal community composition

The archaeal diversity in the anaerobic digester under different operational stages was investigated by constructing mcrA gene clone libraries, and the results are shown in Fig. 5b. In total, 1513 clones were collected after steady-state operation at 20 d HRT. Methanogens (OTU mcrA) from the order Methanosarcinales were predominant in the digester during long-term digestion at 20 d HRT (39.0% of sequences). Specifically, Methanosarcinaceae was the quantitatively dominant aceticlastic group, and are typically thought be mainly responsible for methane production in anaerobic digesters. Methanomicrobiales accounted for 19.1% of the bulk sequences and ranked second among all taxa in the digesters. Methanobacteriaceae was another major sequence within the reactor, accounting for 11.3% of the sequences. Generally, the sequences of Methanomicrobiales and Methanobacteriales were the main hydrogenotrophic methanogens, collectively accounting for 30.4% of the sequences for the sludge digesters. This is similar to the observation that <30% of the methane was derived via the H_2/CO_2 pathway during anaerobic digestion (Sekiguchi and Kamagata, 2004; Hickey et al., 1987). Each of the two Methanopyrales families, Methanomassiliicoccaceae and Methanopyraceae, were also detected, with the former comprising 4.4% of mcrA sequences, and the latter was found in only 2.9%. The minor and unclassified archaea identified in the anaerobic digester accounted for 10.5% of the bulk sequences.

Overall, 965 archaeal OTUs were obtained after 60 days digestion at 10 d HRT. As shown in Fig. 5b, the percentage of Methanosaetaceae decreased from 36.3% to 27.6% for the digester after system collapse, while that of the hydrogenotrophic methanogens of Methanomicrobiales and Methanobacteriales increased slightly (7.9%). Specifically, the Methanomicrobiales increased from 19.1% to 25.8%, and the Methanobacteriales increased from 11.3% to 12.5%. Thus, the hydrogenotrophic methanogens were less sensitive than acetoclastic methanogens, especially with higher levels of VFAs presentation, which is consistent with the observations of Xu et al. (2010). Once the digester recovered after 2 months of digestion at 20 d HRT, the percentages of the Methanomicrobiales and Methanobacteriales declined to the level before deterioration, while that of the Methanosaetaceae increased from 27.6% to 37.6%. The above results clearly demonstrate that aceticlastic Methanosaetaceae increased after depletion of the accumulated VFAs. The percentages of Methanopyrales and Methanomassiliicoccaceae changed insignificantly (ranged 4.40–5.70% for Methanopyrales and 2.60–3.10% for Methanomassiliicoccaceae) during the whole operation. The abundance of genus Methanobacterium did not appear to be affected by the VFA concentration, despite the fact that this genus was predominant within the digesters. In addition, the significant abundance of members of Methanosaetaceae during steady-state digestion implied that they represent the key methanogens during anaerobic digestion of various substrates.


4 Conclusion

Sludge EPS, which is hydrophobic, is mainly composed by proteins rather than carbohydrates. Digestion at 10 d HRT affected hydrolysis of HPO-A, TPI-A related sEPS (especially the hydrophobic proteins), humic acid- and SMP-like substances, which led to the collapse of digesters. Simultaneously significant accumulation of easily biodegradable HPI and carbohydrates in supernatant implied methanogenesis became the rate-limiting factor under such condition. Acetoclastic methanogens were more sensitive than hydrogenotrophic ones at 10 d HRT. In this case, abundance of Firmicutes/Bacteroidetes increased when process collapsed, while Proteobacteria declined from 12.9% to 8.3%. Recovering at 20 d HRT enhanced methanogenesis and biodegradation of accumulated VFAs.
