TITLE: A techno-economic assessment of hydrogen and methane production from the anaerobic treatment of vinasse and glycerol: Single- vs. two-stage process


1 Introduction

Anaerobic digestion (AD) and dark fermentation are increasingly recognized within the framework of the circular economy for their role as biochemical treatment processes enabling the recovery of value-added resources such as bioenergy (H_2/CH_4) and biofertilizers (Kim et al., 2023; Mpofu et al., 2022; Saidi et al., 2023). Additionally, AD effluents following polishing treatment may be utilized as reuse water (Mpofu et al., 2022). Regarding AD products, generated biogas can be converted to heat or electricity on-site or integrated into a natural gas network, and the recovered nutrients (nitrogen, phosphorous, and potassium) can be blended to produce organic mineral fertilizers (Chojnacka and Moustakas, 2024). As a biochemical process that requires lower energy ability to use a wide range of raw materials, AD boasts lower costs compared to thermochemical ones (Saidi et al., 2023). Examples of thermochemical processes that require high temperatures (above 300 °C) and pressure control are pyrolysis, direct combustion, gasification, and hydrothermal liquefaction (Saidi et al., 2023).

As mentioned, anaerobic digestion and dark fermentation are environmentally friendly and cost-effective ways to extract value-added metabolites from waste. In Brazil, two residues that draw attention are those from biofuel production, i.e., vinasse generated in ethanol production (11–15 liters of vinasse per liter of ethanol produced) (Ramos et al., 2022) and glycerol generated in biodiesel production (10 % of all biodiesel produced) (da Costa et al., 2021). Vinasse is currently used in the fertigation process for sugarcane crops. However, due to the composition of vinasse (sulfate, melanoidins, and phenols) and the high volume generated, its excessive release can harm the soil and groundwater (Ramos et al., 2022). The market continues to exhibit a sustained demand for glycerol. On the other hand, due to the increasing use of biodiesel in diesel, particularly in Brazil, and the expensive purification process of this by-product, the price of glycerol has decreased (de Mello et al., 2024). This has led to a search for alternative ways to use both vinasse and glycerol as they are carbon-rich substrates.

Several studies have addressed hydrogen and methane production from vinasse and glycerol separately as an alternative for extracting compounds with high energy content from these wastes, eliminating their organic content in disposal cases (Bernal et al., 2021; da Costa et al., 2021; Dounavis et al., 2015; Rego et al., 2022; Simões et al., 2021). During the dark fermentation of vinasse, high hydrogen production values were observed in anaerobic fluidized bed reactors (AFBRs). Furthermore, this residue has already been co-fermented with glucose, sucrose, and cheese whey, eliminating effluents rich in lactate, acetate, and butanoate (Fuess, 2024). High hydrogen production values were also observed in the glycerol fermentation in AFBR. However, the high acidification of this substrate limited the organic load of the process (Simões et al., 2021). The limitation of the applied load was also observed in producing methane from these wastes, whether in one (da Costa et al., 2021; Ramos et al., 2022) or two-stage AFBR (Ramos et al., 2022). In the case of glycerol, this was due to high acidification, and in the case of vinasse, it was due to the high concentration of its inhibitory compounds (da Costa et al., 2021; Ramos et al., 2022).

Recently, the co-fermentation and co-digestion of vinasse and glycerol were proposed as a solution to nutritionally balance both residues and treat them in a single experimental apparatus, maximizing methane (Borges et al., 2022; Lovato et al., 2019; Takeda et al., 2022) and/or hydrogen production (da Silva et al., 2021). Furthermore, glycerol proved to be an alternative to vinasse in the off-season to ensure the continuity of the reactor (Takeda et al., 2022) and an option to deal with the variability of vinasse composition (Sica et al., 2024). Despite the antagonistic effect of hydrogen production from the co-fermentation of vinasse and glycerol reported in previous studies (da Silva et al., 2021; de Menezes et al., 2023b), in both studies, it was observed that co-fermentation improves the productivity of 1,3-propanediol from glycerol. Additionally, a synergistic effect for methane production can still be obtained in the second-stage AFBR (de Menezes et al., 2023c). This is because methane production from the co-digestion of vinasse and glycerol supported a higher organic loading rate (OLR) (de Menezes et al., 2023a) compared to the mono-digestion of vinasse (Ramos et al., 2021) and glycerol (da Costa et al., 2021), and this occurrence was related to the reduction of the effect of sulfate on methanogenesis (de Menezes et al., 2023a). In this case, the prior acidification of the substrates and the possibility of removing sulfate in a reactor before methanogenesis (de Menezes et al., 2023a) create favorable conditions to maximize methane productivity in two-stage systems (de Menezes et al., 2023c).

de Menezes et al. (2023c) conducted a study comparing different temperature combinations to produce methane from the co-digestion of vinasse and glycerol in two-stage AFBR. The aim was to identify the most efficient combination for OLR fixed at 10 kg-COD/m^3/d. The combinations tested were acidogenic + methanogenic: thermophilic + mesophilic, thermophilic + thermophilic, hyperthermophilic + mesophilic, and hyperthermophilic + hyperthermophilic, respectively. The authors found that the methane production was comparable in thermophilic + mesophilic and thermophilic + thermophilic combinations. However, the authors should have considered the scaling-up implications of reactor heating. Additionally, no application of OLR greater than 10 kg-COD/m^3/d in the co-digestion of vinasse and glycerol in two-stage processes has been identified in the literature. If this information were available, it would help in applying the highest waste concentration in the smallest reactor volume possible.

A significant percentage of the results obtained from laboratory experiments are intended to be used as a basis for implementing experiments on a pilot or industrial scale. However, solely relying on laboratory analyses proves insufficient. To move the system from a laboratory to an industrial scale, a significant investment is required (Kumar et al., 2021). As a result, techno-economic analyses (TEA) play a crucial role in performing a market feasibility study as it gives vital information on the viability and profitability of a new company endeavor. Businesses may make educated judgments about entering new markets or introducing new products by analyzing numerous economic aspects and trends. TEA ensures that every choice is securely founded in facts, reducing the likelihood of expensive setbacks. TEA serves as a guiding light, assisting with important decisions about growing operations, budgeting, pricing strategies, and investment planning.

Therefore, this study aimed at evaluating, on a laboratory scale, the effect of increasing OLR from 5 to 20 kg-COD/m^3/d in thermophilic (55 °C) and mesophilic (25 °C) second-stage AFBRs (HRT of 24 h) fed with sugarcane vinasse and glycerol (1:1 on a COD basis) previously fermented in a thermophilic AFBR (55 °C; HRT of 4 h). Based on the performance data obtained in the experimental assays of this paper and previous ones (de Menezes et al., 2023a, 2023b), this work also assessed the techno-economic feasibility, using the net present value (NPV), internal rate of return (IRR), payback, and risk of financial loss by Monte Carlo simulation, of single- and two-stage systems application on an industrial scale for hydrogen and methane production by the anaerobic co-digestion of vinasse and glycerol.


2 Material and methods

The experimental apparatus and operating conditions for the two-stage co-digestion of sugarcane vinasse and glycerol in AFBRs are depicted in the flowchart ( Fig. 1).


2.1 Thermophilic hydrogenogenic reactor (H 2 -R Thermo )

The hydrogenogenic AFBR (H_2-AFBR_Thermo) start-up and operation were conducted at thermophilic conditions (55 ± 1 °C) as specified by de Menezes et al. (2023b). In the aforementioned study, the authors included information on the vinasse and glycerol samples, as well as the inoculum source and inoculum pre-treatment. Vinasse and glycerol (1:1 ratio on a COD basis) were added to the H_2-AFBR_Thermo at a total organic matter concentration of 10, 15 and 20 g-COD/L at a fixed HRT of 4 h (OLRs of 60, 90, and 120 kg-COD/m^3/d), respectively.


2.2 Thermophilic (CH 4 -R Thermo ) and mesophilic (CH 4 -R Meso ) methanogenic reactors

The AFBRs, with heights and internal diameters of 148 cm and 3.6 cm, respectively, were made of stainless steel with a total individual capacity of 1.55 L. The temperature control in the thermophilic range on CH_4-R_Thermo was carried out by using an ultra-thermostatized bath. Heat exchange to maintain the temperature in 55 ± 1 °C was accomplished by reusing the bath water in the jacketing and the "U"-shaped coil within the reactors. The CH_4-R_Meso was kept at room temperature (25 ± 5 °C).

The support material used to immobilize the biomass in both reactors was a ground tire with a diameter of 2.8–3.5 mm and a density of 1.14 g/cm^3. The recirculation flows of the methanogenic reactors were set at 56 L/h to achieve an upflow velocity 30 % greater than the minimum fluidization velocity of the ground tire (1.18 cm/s).

The inoculum required to start the CH_4-R_Thermo up was collected from a thermophilic upflow anaerobic sludge blanket (UASB) reactor, which is used for sugarcane vinasse treatment in an industry located in the state of São Paulo (Brazil). The thermophilic methanogenic sludge had a pH of 7.7 and total solids and volatile solids contents of 21 and 12 g/L, respectively. The source of the inoculum used to start the CH_4-R_Meso up was the methanogenic sludge of a mesophilic UASB reactor, which is used for poultry slaughterhouse wastewater treatment in an industry located in the state of São Paulo (Brazil). The mesophilic methanogenic sludge had a pH of 7.2 and concentrations of 46 g/L for total solids and 37 g/L for volatile solids, respectively.

At start-up, 272 g of ground tire particles were added to the methanogenic reactors, corresponding to 50 cm of each reactor height (fixed bed). Feeding during the startup period occurred in batch mode. For this purpose, feeding tanks were prepared containing 10 % (v/v) thermophilic methanogenic sludge, 1 g-COD/L (218 mL) of vinasse, 1 g-COD/L (790 mg) of glycerol, 16 g-NaHCO_3 (1 g-NaHCO_3/g-COD/L), and completed with water to a total volume of 8 L. The solutions were purged with N_2 for ten minutes, and then the tanks were connected to the CH_4-R_Thermo and CH_4-R_Meso recirculation pumps. After the consumption of 80 percent of the added organic matter (confirmed by COD analysis) on day 17, the reactors ran in continuous mode.

In continuous mode, the effect of OLR on methane generation from the effluent of the hydrogenogenic reactor was examined by gradually increasing the organic matter concentration from 5 to 20 g-COD/L at a HRT of 24 h. The change in the operational conditions was performed when observing stabilization of methane production and organic matter removal efficiency for 20 consecutive days, that is, a variation of less than 10 % of methane yield (MY) in relation to its mean.


2.3 Analysis

Liquid influent and effluent samples were analyzed for pH and COD, total suspended solids, volatile suspended solids (VSS) (APHA et al., 2017), carbohydrates (Dubois et al., 1956), glycerol (Bondioli and Della Bella, 2005), lactate (Taylor, 1996), volatile fatty acids (DiLallo and Albertson, 1961; Ripley et al., 1986), and sulfate (HACH DR820 Field Test Kit®) concentrations. The concentrations of organic acids and alcohols were determined by high-performance liquid chromatography (Ramos et al., 2021).

The analysis of the gaseous effluent from the reactors consisted of evaluating the volumetric production of biogas by injecting the gas from the reactor’s headspace into a sealed tube through a valve at the bottom, displacing the liquid barrier solution into a container. The amount of gas introduced was determined by measuring the change in liquid levels in the tube and the container (Walker et al., 2009). The composition of the biogas produced was analyzed by gas chromatography (Ramos et al., 2021).


2.4 Scale-up estimation, economic, and risk analyses

(2023a), (2023b). In order to calculate the number of pumps necessary to perform the industrial reactor bed fluidization, the following were considered from the laboratory scale reactors data: height to diameter ratio (Height = 1.48 m; Diameter = 0.036 m; H/D=41.1; Volume = 1.55 L); and bed fluidization velocity (58.0 and 55.2 m/h for acidogenic and methanogenic reactors, respectively).

The assessment was performed considering a sugar and ethanol industry located in São Paulo state (Brazil) with a production of 2×10^9 m³-vinasse/season with an organic matter concentration of 25 g-COD/L. In order to perform co-digestion with glycerol, data from a biodiesel industry close to the sugar and ethanol plant were collected. 3 Results and discussion


3.1 Summary of H 2 -R Thermo  performance

Table 1 displays the effect of raising the OLR from 60 to 90 and 120 kg-COD/m^3/d on the fermentation of vinasse and glycerol (1:1 on a COD basis). More information on the co-fermentation of vinasse and glycerol in thermophilic AFBR is provided by de Menezes et al. (2023b). In summary, at 60 kg-COD/m^3/d, a predilection for glycerol fermentation was identified, and the dismutation of this for the reductive branch resulted in large amounts of 1,3-propanediol (Table 1). At 120 kg-COD/m^3/d, there was a predilection for carbohydrate fermentation when glycerol conversion efficiency decreased. Increased carbohydrate conversion efficiency at 120 kg-COD/m^3/d led to a rise in butyric acid concentration and, consequently, an increase in the volumetric hydrogen production (Table 1).


3.2 Performances of CH 4 -R Thermo  and CH 4 -R Meso

Table 2 shows the performance indicators of second-stage digestion of H_2-R_Thermo effluent in CH_4-R_Thermo and CH_4-R_Meso as a function of the OLRs (5, 7.5, 10, 15, and 20 kg- COD/m^3/d).

The high glycerol consumption efficiencies in CH_4-R_Thermo and CH_4-R_Meso (> 95 %) may be attributable to both the high biodegradability of glycerol and the applied dose of this substrate (remained in the effluent of H_2-R_Thermo). Glycerol enters cells passively, without requiring hydrolysis, and is acidified promptly by microorganisms (Almeida et al., 2022a). In many cases, it is observed that this buildup in volatile acid concentration is deleterious to methanogenic consortia activity (Chou and Su, 2019). Conversely, these side effects are associated with the glycerol concentration and OLR applied (Almeida et al., 2022a). In the case of H_2-R_Thermo, the doses of glycerol applied, in a fixed proportion of vinasse and glycerol, ranged from 0.4 % to 0.8 %_v/v, which is lower than the doses of glycerol associated to a deleterious effect on co-digestion (> 1%_v/v) (Rasit et al., 2019). Consequently, the doses of glycerol fed to CH_4- R_Thermo and CH_4-R_Meso were lower than those fed to H_2-R_Thermo, because H_2-R_Thermo converted 54, 38, and 15 % of glycerol at 60, 90, and 120 kg-COD/m^3/d, respectively.

The mean values of total carbohydrate consumption efficiency remained stable between 66 % and 77 % in CH_4-R_Thermo and between 66 % and 87 % in CH_4-R_Meso. It is worth mentioning that the carbohydrate conversion efficiency was higher in thermophilic single-stage digestion (de Menezes et al., 2023a) when compared to CH_4-R_Thermo, at similar operational conditions (HRT of 4 h and 55 °C). The difference between the performances of CH_4-R_Thermo and single-stage digestion in the efficiency of total carbohydrate removal may be linked to the low concentration of carbohydrates in vinasse. In single-stage digestion, all degradable carbohydrates were converted.

The carbohydrate conversion and organic matter removal efficiencies of CH_4-R_Thermo were lower than those of CH_4-R_Meso at OLRs up to 15 kg-COD/m^3/d (Table 2). This may have occurred due to the greater general diversity of the mesophilic microbiota concerning the thermophilic one (de Menezes et al., 2023c). With the greater diversity of microbiota, the degradation of recalcitrant compounds may have been facilitated in CH_4-R_Meso. Ramos et al. (2021) also observed that organic matter removal efficiencies in mesophilic second-stage UASB (79 %) were higher than those observed in thermophilic second-stage UASB (69 %) at 18 kg- COD/m^3/d. The better performance of mesophilic reactors compared to thermophilic reactors is associated with the characteristics of the substrate and usually occurs when the hydrolysis rate is not a limiting factor (Chen et al., 2020). The performances observed by Ramos et al. (2021) in two-stage vinasse mono-digestion and CH_4-R_Meso concerning CH_4-R_Thermo indicate that vinasse hydrolysis is not a limiting factor for the digestion of this substrate. Furthermore, the higher organic matter removal values of CH_4-R_Meso (81.8 %) with the mesophilic UASB of Ramos et al. (2021) (79 %) can be attributed to the favoring of mesophilic digestion due to the increase in biodegradability conferred by the addition of glycerol.

A negative effect of increasing substrate biodegradability can be observed at an OLR of 20 kg-COD/m^3/d in CH_4-R_Meso (Table 2). The high substrate conversion rate in a mesophilic reactor can increase the variability of liquid-phase metabolites. However, this increase in acid generation by the acidogenic consortium interfered in the rate of degradation by the methanogens (da Costa et al., 2021), causing acids to accumulate. In fact, the VFA concentration in CH_4-R_Meso was 1279 mg/L compared to 682 mg/L in CH_4-R_Thermo. Therefore, acids may have accumulated and inhibited methanogenic activity in CH_4-R_Meso, raising the ratio between intermediate alkalinity and partial alkalinity (IA/PA) to 0.90 at 20 kg-COD/m^3/d. In contrast, a higher degradation rate and low variety of metabolites synthesized under thermophilic conditions might explain the organic matter removal efficiency of 79 % and the IA/PA ratio of 0.42 in CH_4-R_Thermo at 20 kg-COD/m^3/d (Begum et al., 2020; Zhang et al., 2020).

At OLRs higher than 10 kg-COD/m^3/d, acetic and propionic acids were found (Table 2). Therefore, CH_4-R_Thermo and CH_4-R_Meso degraded all 1,3-propanediol generated by the H_2- R_Thermo (Table 1) from 5 to 15 kg-COD/m^3/d. According to reports, 1,3-propanediol to methane in a second-stage reactor is challenging (Sun et al., 2018), but sulfate reduction may have aided in the degradation of 1,3-propanediol, as its degradation to acetate requires the reduction of electron acceptors such as sulfate (Oppenberg and Schink, 1990). Under all operating conditions, the sulfate influent on CH_4-R_Thermo and CH_4-R_Meso (Table 2) was completely eliminated.

The rise of sulfate and 1,3-propanediol influent concentrations (Table 1) may have been responsible for the divergent behavior of the methane production rate (MPR) of both reactors. The MPR linearly increased to 4.53 L-CH_4/L/d at 20 kg-COD/m^3/d in CH_4-R_Thermo and to 3.85 L-CH_4/L/d at 15 kg-COD/m^3/d in CH_4-R_Meso. The consumption of organic matter in sulfidogenesis is configured as a competitive pathway for substrate as it is no longer directed to methane production (Kiyuna et al., 2017). However, one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) showed that there was no significant variation in the values of organic matter removal efficiency and MY as a function of the OLR since F (0.2937) < F_critical (1.718165) and p-value (0.997) > 0.05.

Ramos et al. (2021) performed the mono-digestion of vinasse in two-stage AFBRs and observed a reduction in the average values of MY from 270 to 170 and 150 NmL- CH_4/g- COD_removed when increasing the OLR of 8–18 and 24.7 kg-COD/m^3/d, respectively. The MY values achieved were significantly lower than those observed in the CH_4- R_Thermo and CH_4-R_Meso (Table 2).


3.3 Comparison of single- and two-stage systems on a laboratory scale

The following systems were compared:(i) Thermophilic single-stage system fed with vinasse and glycerol (1:1 on a COD basis) with influent concentration of 10, 15 and 20 g-COD/L and HRT of 24 h (Data from de Menezes et al. 2023a); (ii) Two-stage system including H_2-R_Thermo (fed with 50 % vinasse and 50 % glycerol with influent concentration of 10, 15 and 20 g-COD/L and HRT of 4 h) and CH_4-R_Thermo (fed with undiluted acidogenic reactor effluent and HRT of 24 h) (Data from de Menezes et al. 2023b and this study); and (iii) Two-stage system including H_2-R_Thermo (fed with 50 % vinasse and 50 % glycerol with influent concentration of 10, 15 and 20 g-COD/L and HRT of 4 h) and CH_4-R_Meso (fed with undiluted acidogenic reactor effluent and HRT of 24 h) (Data from de Menezes et al. 2023b and this study).

Table 3 shows the comparison of results among systems and Fig. 2 illustrates calculations for when influent COD was 20 g-COD/L (figures illustrating all scenarios can be found in the Supplementary Material). The main indicator was the energetic yield (γ), that is the total energy produced by the systems divided by the total organic matter removed. Regardless of the influent COD, the single-stage system achieved a better yield than the two-stage system. The best overall energetic yield was achieved by the thermophilic single-stage system fed with influent at 10 g-COD/L (9.7 kJ/g-COD), which is 9 % higher than the yield achieved by the thermophilic + mesophilic two-stage system and 25 % higher than the thermophilic + thermophilic two-stage system.

The best energy yield achieved by the thermophilic single-stage system in comparison with the two-stage system can be explained by the poor performance of the H_2-R_Thermo, which achieved a low hydrogen productivity while using some of the organic matter, resulting in a decreased influent COD for the second-stage methanogenic reactor. This result is in accordance with Lovato et al. (2020), who stated that a two-stage system only achieves a higher energetic yield than the single-stage system when the latter has a methane yield lower than 200 L- CH_4/kg- COD_removed, which occurs when the influent has significantly high organic loads, hazardous, and/or inhibiting components, which is not the case of this study.


3.4 Comparison of single and two-stage systems on an industrial scale

For the scale-up estimation, firstly the calculation of the daily flow of wastewaters for the treatment system was performed. For vinasse, the daily volumetric flow rate was 5480 m³- vinasse/d with a COD concentration of 25 kg-COD/m^3. For glycerol, the daily volumetric flow rate was 90 m³-glycerol/d with a COD concentration of 1520 kg-COD/m^3. The combination of these wastewaters generated an influent with 5570 m³/d and 49 kg-COD/m^3 (50:50 on a COD basis and 98:02 v/v). Therefore, the COD flow entering the system was 3.17 kg-COD/s. Fig. 3 and Table 4 present the scale-up results. It was only possible to perform the estimation for scenarios when influent COD was 20 g-COD/L, because vinasse volumetric flow was too large to dilute, resulting in unrealistic reactor volumes.

The reactor of the single-stage system (HRT of 24 h) was operated at 22.1 kg-COD/m^3/d with an influent flow rate of 0.16 m³/s, which is the combination of the mixture wastewaters flow rate (0.064 m³/s with a concentration of 49.2 kg-COD/m^3) plus the effluent recirculation (0.10 m³/s with a concentration of 4.7 kg-COD/m^3) used to dilute the mixture of industrial wastewaters and reach the desired concentration of 22.1 kg-COD/m^3. In order to achieve a removed organic loading rate of 17.4 kg-COD/m^3/d with this COD flow rate, an industrial scale reactor with a volume of 17,820 m³ would be needed. For bed fluidization, 600 stainless steel tubes with a height of 40 m and a diameter of 1.0 m would be required. Each tube would be fed with 0.11 L/s (total flow rate of 0.16 m³/s) and with bed fluidization velocity of 11.1 L/s. The system would then produce 33.5 mol-CH_4/s and have a global energetic yield of 9.4 MJ/kg- COD.

In the two-stage system (HRT: first stage of 4 h and second-stage of 24 h) composed by thermophilic reactors, influent dilution was performed with the thermophilic methanogenic reactor effluent (0.10 m³/s with a concentration of 4.4 kg-COD/m^3), which reduced the volume of the industrial acidogenic reactor to 2940 m³. However, the COD flow rate leaving this reactor was still high (0.16 m³/s with a concentration of 21.2 kg-COD/m^3), which increased the thermophilic methanogenic reactor volume to 17,620 m³. The two-stage system would then produce 1.57 mol-H_2/s and 33.0 mol-CH_4/s, achieving a global energetic yield of 9.3 MJ/kg- COD, which is not equal to the one achieved on a laboratory scale (7.9 MJ/kg- COD). The reason for the different values is due to the scale-up procedure, which considers the removed OLR as the key parameter. As the OLR_Removed is the same on both scales, this causes a difference of reactor volumes that decreases the acidogenic reactor volume in comparison to the methanogenic one (which are equal on a laboratory scale). In this study, the acidogenic reactor that performed poorly was the main factor for the low energetic yield achieved. Therefore, if on an industrial scale, the acidogenic reactor volume is decreased, this will lead to a better two-stage energetic yield.

Almeida et al. (2022b) used similar reactor configurations to those of the present work (thermophilic AFBR, OLR_A of 120 kg-COD/m³/d, and HRT of 4 h) in the co-fermentation of glycerol and cheese whey. The authors obtained better performance indicators in terms of hydrogen production and organic matter removal. The combination of effluent rich in lipids (glycerol) and effluent with low carbohydrate content (vinasse) appears as one of the determining factors that limited the performance of the hydrogenogenic reactor and, consequently, of the two-stage system.

In the two-stage system with the mesophilic methanogenic reactor, the same procedure as before was performed. The biogas production would be of 1.75 mol-H_2/s and 31.2 mol- CH_4/s, achieving a global energetic yield of 9.4 MJ/kg-COD. Regarding only the energetic yield and organic matter removal efficiencies, all three scenarios achieved close results.


3.5 Economic and risk analyses

Considering the estimated reactor volumes, energy yield and production from the combustion of H_2 and CH_4, it the total investment costs for the best conditions of single- and two-stage thermophilic and mesophilic systems were calculated.

One storage tank for glycerol was considered, with volume equal to 630 m³. This volume tank was calculated by the total volume of glycerol needed in four months of operation. Furthermore, no vinasse tank was considered in this analysis as vinasse is usually stored in large stabilization ponds at ethanol plants. In addition, its production is too large, which would require several storage tanks, increasing costs. The total costs comprised one and four feeding pumps for glycerol and vinasse, respectively, as well as recirculation pumps for each tube.

The economic parameter values and equipment costs of the single- and two-stage systems are presented in Table 5, as well as the values of the single- and two-stage systems’ direct, indirect costs, and fees. The single-stage system tanks and reactor were 15 and 27 % cheaper than thermophilic + thermophilic and thermophilic + mesophilic equipment, which is due to the addition of the acidogenic reactor. The addition of the extra acidogenic reactor also increases the total number of pumps necessary for bed fluidization, incurring costs. Consequently, the costs for installation (47 % of Ec), instrumentation and control (36 % of Ec), pipes and valves (68 % of Ec), electricity (11 % of Ec), engineering (33 % of Ec), and fees and contingencies (66 % of Ec) also increase because they are calculated based on the total equipment cost. This resulted in the single-stage system having a total investment cost 15 % lower than the thermophilic + thermophilic system and 28 % lower than the thermophilic + mesophilic system (USD 45 M, 52 M, and 58 M, respectively).

The results obtained in this paper are in accordance with Batista et al. (2023), who also investigated hydrogen and methane production in cheese whey treatment in single- and two-stage systems but using anaerobic sequencing batch biofilm reactors (AnSBBR). In the scale-up estimation and economic analysis, the authors concluded that higher investment costs are necessary for the two-stage system than for the single-stage system (USD 470 k and 272 k, respectively). It should be noted that the primary limits of comparing economic evaluations between studies are linked to the method of determining fixed expenses (direct and indirect) and working capital. Empirical equations (Turton et al., 2012) or the Lang factor may be adopted (Batista et al. 2023 also used the Lang factor method). Furthermore, fees and taxes vary depending on location and time. However, there are certain commonalities, such as the cost of major items in a plant.

An acidogenic reactor does not necessarily impair the economic feasibility of every process. The two-stage system (thermophilic + mesophilic) from Almeida et al. (2023a) presented practically equal economic indicators (NPV, IRR, and payback) than those of the single-stage system (thermophilic). The acidogenic reactor (OLR_A of 120 kg-COD/m^3/d) studied by Almeida et al. (2023b), compared to the acidogenic reactor of the present study, achieved higher values of removed OLR (11.6 vs. 5.7 kg-COD/m^3/d) and HY (9.85 vs. 0.29 mol-H_2/kg-COD), resulting in smaller volume and lower costs for the second-stage methanogenic reactor. The better yield achieved by their reactor also improved the system’s energy generation, improving its financial return.

The net present value, internal rate of return, and payback for each scenario were calculated, which are also presented in Table 5. The three scenarios revealed similar economic indicators and the resulting three IRR values were higher than the minimum acceptable rate of return (MARR), which is equal to 12 % (Volpi et al., 2023), showing that the deployment of all systems can be profitable.

The best economic indicators were found in the single-stage system (USD 58 M of NPV, 18.3 % of IRR, and 9.5 years of payback). It presented NPV values 23 and 82 % higher than the two-stage systems (thermophilic + thermophilic and thermophilic + mesophilic, respectively). In addition to the higher investment return, single-stage systems are much simpler to operate than the two-stage ones (Rajendran et al., 2020).

To be competitive and economically feasible compared to the single-stage process, CH_4 production in CH_4-R_Thermo should rise from 2848 to 3298 kmol-CH_4/d, approximately 16 %. This methane production is equivalent to an increase in methane yield from 12.1 to 14.0 mol- CH_4/kg-COD. In this scenario, both systems would achieve the same internal rate of return of 18 %. Nevertheless, the two-stage initial investment would still be 15 % higher than the single-stage. Considering the increase only in H_2 production in H_2-R_Thermo would lead to a higher yield than the theoretical one for both systems, achieving the same internal rate of return.

The viability of achieving a yield of the second stage 16 % higher than the experimental is difficult to predict, due to the lack of studies regarding the two-stage co-digestion of glycerin and vinasse. Ramos et al. (2022), who treated vinasse in two-stage AFBRs, achieved a methane yield of 7.8 mol-CH_4/kg-COD_removed in the second stage at an OLR_A of 18 kg-COD/m³/d. This value may indicate the improbability of two-stage being better than single-stage. Nevertheless, the co-digestion strategy could improve the yield value. The yield achieved by Lovato et al. (2020), who treated whey and glycerin in mesophilic two-stage Anaerobic Sequencing Batch Biofilm reactors, was 15.5 mol-CH_4/kg-COD_removed. With a high yield of 14.0 mol- CH_4/kg- COD_removed, the treatment of whey and glycerin using a thermophilic acidogenic AFBR and a mesophilic methanogenic AFBR from Almeida et al. (2023b) also showed positive performance, leading the single- and two-stage to exhibit nearly equal economic indicators.

Furthermore, the single-stage payback is 9.5 years, which represents almost 1/3 of the plant's lifetime. Therefore, considering that the single-stage system presented better performance and economic indicators compared to the two-stage system, the risk analysis using a Monte Carlo simulation was carried out only for the single-stage scenario.

The Monte Carlo simulation showed that the risk associated with a negative NPV is 12 %. However, the probability of reaching a NPV between zero and the estimated NPV in the baseline scenario (USD 58 M) was 59 %. The probability distribution graph ( Fig. 4) indicates in detail the relative probability of obtaining each NPV. The achievement of NPV values of USD 50 M ≤ NPV < USD 100 M; USD 100 M ≤ NPV < USD 150 M; and NPV ≥ USD 150 M had a probability of 25; 7; and 2 %, respectively. Thus, the risk analysis suggested a very low probability of the project's failure to present a financial return, but that a sudden change in the key parameters might result in a lower return than calculated in the current scenario.

According to Granjo et al. (2017), the main contributors to the overall risk are the uncertainties in the price of the products, the cost of utilities, such as water, steam, and electricity, and mainly in the cost of raw materials, which contributed to 89 % of the manufacturing cost. Since the techno-economic analysis of the present study considered vinasse and glycerol as substrates for hydrogen and methane production, the costs spent on raw materials were not accounted for. Therefore, the risk presented here is considerably lower.

It is worth noting that the economic analysis presented here did not consider carbon credit sales, because Brazil's carbon credit value is not fully set, and the emissions trading system is under discussion. Furthermore, a life cycle assessment would be necessary to determine the total prevented carbon dioxide emissions compared to the present system, which is highly complex and is not the objective of this study. Nevertheless, the carbon credits sale could further improve the baseline scenario and reduce the risk associated with changes in the key factors, as the project has the potential to generate renewable energy (The World Bank, 2022).

Fig. 5 depicts the various NPV distributions for the single-stage scenario, where the curve labeled “All variables” considers all the uncertain key factors simultaneously, and each of the remaining curves considers the individual influence of varying each key parameter and maintains the others at their baseline values. This figure demonstrates that the capitalization rate, inflation, and the electricity sales price are the three most impactful variables in shifting the NPV, respectively. In the case of individual variation in the capitalization rate, a decrease in the value of this parameter would allow an increase in the NPV to above USD 24 M. Although a single decrease in the electricity sales price could result in a negative NPV (- USD 7.2 M), a rise in this parameter would have a positive influence on the NPV, but other areas and companies would likely be affected.


4 Conclusions

The present study experimentally evaluated the production of hydrogen and methane in thermophilic and mesophilic second-stage AFBRs fed with sugarcane vinasse and glycerol. The scale-up and techno-economic feasibility of single- and two-stage systems fed with the aforementioned wastewaters were assessed.

In thermophilic + thermophilic two-stage anaerobic digestion, the rise of the OLR to 20 kg-COD/m^3/d induced an increase in methane production (4.53 L-CH_4/L/d) with steady organic matter removal (79.2 %) and no accumulation of acids. The same trend was observed in two-stage thermophilic + mesophilic digestion up to 15 kg-COD/m^3/d (3.85 L-CH_4/L/d and 81.8 %). However, the quick acidification of substrates in the mesophilic methanogenic reactor led to an imbalance between acid production and consumption, and both organic matter removal and methane production decreased at 20 kg-COD/m^3/d (3.85 L-CH_4/L/d and 66.1 %), thus thermophilic + thermophilic two-stage digestion outperformed thermophilic + mesophilic two-stage digestion. The difference between the thermophilic single-stage reactor and the second-stage methanogenic thermophilic reactor was not significant in terms of organic matter removal and energy production at a laboratory scale (78.6/79.2 % and 4.55/4.53 L-CH_4/L/d, respectively).

According to scale-up estimates, a thermophilic single-stage system capable of treating 2×10^9 m³-vinasse/season would require a 17,820 m³ reactor. Moreover, 26.89 MW could be generated by burning the methane produced. The estimated initial investment for this system was USD 45 M, with a net present value of USD 58 M and an internal rate of return of 18 %. The best-performing two-stage system (thermophilic + thermophilic) would achieve energy production similar to that of the single-stage (26.84 MW) and would need an acidogenic reactor of 2940 m³ and a methanogenic reactor of 17,620 m³ (totaling 20,560 m³). The prospective result is a higher initial investment of USD 51.9 M and a lower potential return, with a NPV of USD 47 M and IRR of 16 %. As a result, the economic analysis suggests that the thermophilic single-stage system was preferable to a two-stage system. The addition of an acidogenic reactor contributes minimally to organic matter removal and bioenergy production, making the single-stage the smallest and most energy-efficient system. The two-stage system would be preferable only if the single-stage system had poor overall performance. Therefore, the CH_4 production in the thermophilic methanogenic reactor of the two-stage system must be increased by 16 % to be competitive and economically feasible compared to the single-stage system. In this scenario, both systems would achieve the same internal rate of return of 18 %, nevertheless the initial investment of the two-stage system would still be 15 % higher than that of the single-stage system. A Monte Carlo simulation for implementing the industrial single-stage system revealed a 12 % financial loss risk, and showed that the capitalization rate, inflation, and the electricity sales price are the three most significant variables that affect the NPV.

Therefore, the current study attempted to demonstrate that using anaerobic digestion technology for vinasse and glycerol treatment would help reduce the environmental impact of improper waste disposal while producing electricity, and that the single-stage system may provide more energy and economic benefits than the two-stage system. Future works are recommended to perform a life cycle assessment of the industrial process to calculate the prevented carbon dioxide emissions, gaining quantifiable information about the decreased global warming potential of the suggested technology.
