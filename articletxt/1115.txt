TITLE: Synergistic pretreatment of waste activated sludge using CaO2 in combination with microwave irradiation to enhance methane production during anaerobic digestion


1 Introduction

Energy demands and their economic and environmental consequences urgently require a solution. Conventional biological wastewater treatment processes produce vast amounts of waste activated sludge (WAS), which is difficult and expensive to dispose. However, WAS can be a better option for generating energy because it contains abundant carbon and nutrients [1]. Among several sludge treatment methods, anaerobic digestion (AD) for methane production is a preferred because it is able to reduce the volume and weight of sludge significantly and produce renewable energy (i.e., biogas) simultaneously, as well as to reduce the treatment costs [2]. It is estimated that the methane produced by AD could be used to provide 70–80% of the energy consumed by waste water treatment plants (WWTPs) if the digestion efficiency is maximized [3]. However, application of AD has been limited because of its long retention time (20–50 days) and low degradation efficiency (20–50%), which are generally associated with the slow hydrolysis of WAS [4]. Cell membrane and extracellular polymeric substances (EPS) have been shown to present physical and chemical barriers to direct anaerobic degradation, respectively [5]. Thus, various sludge pretreatment technologies, such as thermal, chemical, mechanical, ultrasonic, enzymatic methods and their combinations have been developed to overcome the limitations [6–9].

In recent years, microwave (MW) irradiation has gained widespread popularity as an effective and environmentally friendly method for sludge treatment, mainly due to its rapid and selective heating, energy efficiency, capacity to enhance the yield and quality of products and diminish hazardous product formation and emissions [10–15]. MW technology is capable of disrupting sludge flocs and cells and releasing organic matter into the soluble phase [12,16–18]. In conventional heating, heat penetrates materials from outside to inside, while microwaves heat materials internally, thus minimizing heat loss during transmission and improving treatment efficiency [12]. Additionally, MW irradiation includes a thermal effect and an athermal effect. The “athermal effect” generally refers to an effect that is not associated with an increase in temperature [19], and is caused by the polarized parts of macromolecules aligning with the poles of the electromagnetic field, resulting in the possible breakage of hydrogen bonds [20]. The “thermal effect” is caused by the absorption of MW energy by water or organic complexes that are marked by either constant or induced polarization [21]. MW pretreatment has been shown to be the most effective method for enhancing methane potential of pulp mill waste sludge [22,23]. Thus, MW pretreatment can be used as a technology for energy recovery from WAS.

MW technology can not only be utilized as a stand-alone pretreatment process for WAS, but also combined with other methods to further improvement treatment efficiency [12]. H_2O_2, the reagent most commonly used for oxidation, has been shown to synergistically enhance the solubilization of sludge when combined with MW [10,18,21,24]. Moreover, some studies have indicated that combined MW/alkali treatment might act synergistically and become an energy-efficient and cost effective alternative for sludge treatment [11,16,17]. Chang et al. [17] reported that combined MW/NaOH treatment synergistically enhanced sludge solubilization and achieved 46% COD solubilization, which was 20% greater than the additive value of MW and alkali alone (26.5%).

The application of calcium peroxide (CaO_2) in WAS pretreatment is a new and promising technology for resource recovery from sludge. Li et al. [25] found that the hydrolysis and acidification of WAS were significantly enhanced with the addition of CaO_2; moreover, CaO_2 oxidized most refractory organic contaminants that were difficult to biodegrade under ordinary anaerobic conditions. Zhang et al. [26] demonstrated that CaO_2 treatment can be used as a promising technology for the removal of phenolic endocrine disrupting compounds from WAS and to facilitate WAS reuse. However, the production of methane was inhibited by CaO_2 addition [25]. To solve this problem, this technology should be improved.

CaO_2 dissolves in water to form H_2O_2 and Ca(OH)_2 (Eq. (1)): FORMULA: CaO 2 + 2 H 2 O → H 2 O 2 + Ca ( OH ) 2

As the combination of MW with either H_2O_2 or alkali has synergistic effects on WAS solubilization, the combination of MW with CaO_2 should have stronger effects than either alone. However, it is a complicated system that involves heat, electromagnetic, alkaline and oxidation effects. Therefore, it is of interest to investigate whether synergism occurs among these effects and if CaO_2/MW pretreatment can facilitate methane production during the subsequent AD process.

The present study investigated the effects of combined CaO_2 and MW pretreatments on the anaerobic digestion of WAS. Soluble chemical oxygen demand (SCOD), total solids (TS) and volatile suspended solids (VSS) were measured to evaluate the effects on sludge solubilization. Additionally, short-chain fatty acids (SCFAs) and methane production were tested to assess the effects on AD, while capillary suction time (CST) and specific resistance to filtration (SRF) were measured to evaluate the effects on dewaterability. To understand the mechanism of this process, sole MW, CaO_2, Ca(OH)_2, and H_2O_2 treatment were also conducted as comparison and the hydroxyl radical (^OH) was quantified. Moreover, the microbial activity and archaea community structure were assayed to clarify the microbial action.


2 Materials and methods


2.1 Chemicals

CaO_2 (80%) and Ca(OH)_2 (95%) were purchased from Fisher (USA). Liquid H_2O_2 (A.R., 30%, w/w) was obtained from Sinopharm Chemical Reagent (Shanghai, China). All high-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC) grade solvents, including acetonitrile and sulfuric acid, were purchased from Sigma-Aldrich (USA). The water used for HPLC analysis was purified using a Milli-Q system (Millipore, USA). 4-Chlorobenzoicacid (p-CBA) was purchased from Sigma-Aldrich (>98% pure, USA). Other chemicals used in the experiments were of analytical grade and were obtained from Sinopharm Chemical Reagent (Shanghai, China).


2.2 Waste activated sludge characterization

The WAS was withdrawn from the secondary sedimentation tank of a full-scale sewage treatment plant in Shanghai, China. This plant is designed to treat municipal wastewater using an anaerobic-aerobic process. The influent flow rate of the WWTP is 75,000 m^3/d, and the service population is 200,000. The sludge retention time (SRT) of the system is 8–10 d. The sludge was filtered with a 1 mm × 1 mm screen and concentrated by settling for approximately 24 h at 4 °C prior to use. The main characteristics (average data plus standard deviations of triplicate analyses) of the concentrated sludge samples were as follows: pH, 6.9 ± 0.1; total chemical oxygen demand (TCOD), 9290 ± 130 mg/L; SCOD, 120 ± 10 mg/L; TS, 12,950 ± 320 mg/L; VSS, 8620 ± 122 g/L; total protein (as COD), 4980 ± 68 mg/L; total carbohydrate (as COD), 1012 ± 49 mg/L.


2.3 Pretreatment for waste activated sludge

CaO_2 oxidation combined with MW irradiation was used for pretreatment of WAS. A domestic microwave apparatus (Galanz, P70D20TL-D4, China) with a maximum power output of 700 W at a frequency of 2450 MHz was used. CaO_2 and MW treatments were combined in two different sequences: CaO_2/MW or MW/CaO_2 treatment. For CaO_2/MW treatment, 450 mL of sludge were first mixed well with CaO_2 for 2 min, then exposed to MW irradiation, after which the treated sludge samples were cooled to room temperature for subsequent use. For MW/CaO_2 treatment, the sludge samples were first subjected to MW treatment, then cooled to room temperature before mixing with CaO_2. A set of tests with 27 runs (each with three replicates) was conducted to select the optimal pretreatment conditions for methane production in the subsequent AD (Table 1 ). Experiments were designed using three levels of CaO_2 (0.05, 0.1, 0.2 g/g VSS), MW power (160, 320, 480 W), and MW irradiation time (2, 4, 6 min). The temperature of the samples was measured immediately after they were exposed to MW irradiation. The optimal condition was selected according to methane production in the subsequent AD process. Moreover, since Ca(OH)_2 and H_2O_2 can be produced from CaO_2, the effects of Ca(OH)_2 and H_2O_2 were also investigated. The dosages of Ca(OH)_2 (0.05 g/gVSS) and H_2O_2 (0.048 g/gVSS) were calculated according to their stoichiometric release from 0.1 gCaO_2/gVSS shown in Eq. (1). When combined with Ca(OH)_2 or H_2O_2, the MW output power and irradiation time were 480 W and 2 min, respectively.


2.4 Batch anaerobic digestion tests

The pretreated sludge samples were further treated by AD. AD experiments were conducted in a series of 600 mL glass serum bottles at working sludge volumes of 450 mL. Control tests were performed using the sludge samples without any pretreatment. After adding the sludge samples, each bottle was purged with nitrogen gas to remove oxygen. The bottles were then immediately capped with a rubber stopper and placed in an air-bath shaker (200 rpm) under mesophilic conditions (35 ± 1 °C). Sludge and gas samples were collected at preset times. All AD tests were conducted in triplicate. The values of the results used for t-test were mean of triplicates (n = 3) ± the standard deviation.


2.5 Chemical analysis

TCOD, SCOD, VSS, and TS were measured according to the Standard Methods [27]. Sludge solubilization can describe the transfer of COD from the particulate fraction to the liquid fraction of sludge. Solubilization improvement (%) was calculated using the following equation: FORMULA: Solubilization improvement ( % ) = SCOD ( pretreatment ) - SCOD ( control ) TCOD × 100 % where SCOD (pretreatment) is the SCOD of the sludge sample after pretreatment, SCOD (control) is the SCOD of the sludge sample in the control tests, TCOD is the total COD of the raw sludge sample.

The biogas was collected by releasing the pressure in the bottles using a glass syringe (100 mL) to equilibrate with the room pressure, and the CH_4 and CO_2 concentrations were measured using a gas chromatograph GC-14B (Shimadzu, Japan) equipped with a thermal conductivity detector and a 3 m stainless column. The injection, column, and detector temperatures were 40 °C, 50 °C and 90 °C, respectively, and nitrogen was applied as the carrier gas at a flow rate of 30 mL/min. The H_2 concentration was analyzed via a gastight syringe with a 0.2 mL injection volume and a gas chromatograph (GC112A, China) equipped with a thermal conductivity detector and a 4 mm × 32 m stainless column. The temperatures of the injection port, column, and detector were set at 40, 40, and 80 °C, respectively, and nitrogen was applied as the carrier gas at a flow rate of 30 mL/min [28,29].

The SCFAs were analyzed using an Agilent 6890 GC gas chromatograph (Agilent, USA) equipped with a flame-ionization detector (FID) and an automatic liquid sampler as described in our previous study [25]. The total SCFAs (TSCFAs) content was calculated as the sum of the above measured SCFAs. The SCFAs concentrations were converted to COD using their stoichiometric conversion factors (1.067 for acetic acid, 1.514 for propionic acid, 1.818 for butyric and iso-butyric acids and 2.039 for valeric and iso-valeric acids) [30,31].

CST was measured using a CST meter 304 M (Triton, England) with a single-radius test head and CST paper (size: 7 × 9 cm). The CST measurement corresponds to the time needed for water to cross a fixed distance in the filter paper. The method consists of injecting a sludge sample (5 mL) into a small cylinder placed on a sheet of chromatography paper. While the paper extracts liquid from the sludge by capillary suction, water released from the sludge travels between two contact points on the chromatography paper and the travel time (in seconds) is then recorded by a digital timer. In this study, the sludge temperature was kept constant at 22 °C since variations in temperature can affect CST results [10]. The specific resistance to filtration (SRF) tests were performed using a Buchner Funnel with a Whatman # 2 filter paper while applying 2 bar of vacuum suction [32].


2.6 Quantification of hydroxyl radical ( 
                            
                         OH)

To explore the synergistic effects of MW and CaO_2, the hydroxyl radical (^OH) was quantified as described by Dong and Rosario-Ortiz [33]. p-CBA was always used as a trap substance for ^OH, so the quantitative determination of p-CBA could be used to characterize the ^OH concentration. First, the p-CBA (100 mg/L) was added and mixed well with the sludge samples, after which the samples were treated with CaO_2 (0.1 g/gVSS) or MW irradiation (480 W, 2 min). The sludge supernatant samples were measured after filtering through a 0.45 μm filter (Sartorius). An HPLC equipped with a reversed-phase C-18 column (4.6 mm × 250 mm, 5 mm, Agilent, USA) was used for p-CBA analysis. The injection volume was 10 μL and the flow rate was 1 mL/min. p-CBA was detected at 240 nm at a temperature of 30 °C. The mobile phase was 50% acetonitrile and 50% 5 mM sulfuric acid. The average value of the three repeated tests was presented.


2.7 Analysis of the key enzyme activity

The activity of protease, α-amylase, phosphotransacetylase (PTA), acetate kinase (AK) and coenzyme F_420 was tested according to the methods described in our previous study [25]. All enzyme activity assays were conducted in triplicate and the mean values and standard deviations were recorded.


2.8 Microbial community characterization


2.8.1 DNA extraction and PCR amplification

Microbial DNA was extracted from the sludge of three replicate tests using an E.Z.N.A.® Tissue DNA Kit (Omega Bio-tek, Norcross, GA, USA) according to the manufacturer’s protocols, then pooled together. Real-time PCR assays were conducted using the following primers specific for Archaea: Arch344F (5′-ACGGGGYGCAGCAGGCGCGA-3′) and Arch915R (5′-GTGCTCCCCCGCCAATTCCT-3′). The thermal program used for amplification of the 16rRNA gene was as follows: 95 °C for 2 min, followed by 25 cycles of 95 °C for 30 s, 55 °C for 30 s, and 72 °C for 30 s and then final extension at 72 °C for 5 min. PCR reactions were performed in triplicate in 20 μL mixtures containing 4 μL of 5 × FastPfu Buffer, 2 μL of 2.5 mM dNTPs, 0.8 μL of each primer (5 μM), 0.4 μL of FastPfu Polymerase, and 10 ng of template DNA.


2.8.2 Illumina MiSeq sequencing

Amplicons were extracted from 2% agarose gels and purified using the AxyPrep DNA Gel Extraction Kit (Axygen Biosciences, Union City, CA, USA) according to the manufacturer’s instructions, then quantified using QuantiFluor™ -ST (Promega, USA). Purified amplicons were pooled in equimolar concentrations and paired-end sequenced (2 × 250) on an Illumina MiSeq platform according to the standard protocols. The raw reads were deposited into the NCBI Sequence Read Archive (SRA) database under accession numbers PRJNA309216 and PRJNA310800, respectively.


2.8.3 Processing of sequencing data

Raw fastq files were demultiplexed, then quality-filtered using QIIME (version 1.17). QIIME is an open-source bioinformatics pipeline for performing microbiome analysis from raw DNA sequencing data. QIIME is designed to take users from raw sequencing data generated on the Illumina or other platforms through publication quality graphics and statistics [34]. The criteria are as following: (i) The 250 bp reads were truncated at any site receiving an average quality score <20 over a 10 bp sliding window, while truncated reads that were shorter than 50 bp were discarded; (ii) exact barcode matching, two nucleotide mismatch in primer matching, reads containing ambiguous characters were removed; (iii) only sequences that overlap by longer than 10 bp were assembled according to their overlap sequence. Reads that could not be assembled were discarded. Operational Taxonomic Units (OTUs) were clustered with a 97% similarity cutoff using UPARSE (version 7.1 http://drive5.com/uparse/) and chimeric sequences were identified and removed using UCHIME. UPARSE works by quality-filtering reads, trimming them to a fixed length, optionally discarding singleton reads and then clustering the remaining reads [35]. UCHIME detects chimeric sequences with two or more segments, UCHIME either uses a database of chimera-free sequences or detects chimeras de novo by exploiting abundance data [36]. The phylogenetic affiliation of each 16S rRNA gene sequence was analyzed by RDP Classifier (http://rdp.cme.msu.edu/) against the silva (SSU115) 16S rRNA database using a confidence threshold of 70%.


3 Results and discussion


3.1 Investigation of CaO 2 /MW pretreatment conditions

Both CaO_2 dose and MW irradiation influenced the methane production in the subsequent AD process (Table 1). At the same CaO_2 dose, both MW irradiation intensity and MW contact time had significant effects on the subsequent methane production. A higher MW irradiation output power and a longer irradiation time led to a higher temperature of WAS (Table 1). However, higher microwave irradiation intensity or longer contact time might be unfavorable for subsequent AD. At CaO_2 doses of 0.05, 0.1 and 0.2 g/gVSS, the highest cumulative methane production (CMP) was observed at a MW irradiation of 480 W/2 min, 320 W/4 min and 480 W/2 min, respectively. This is because higher microwave irradiation energy with a longer contact time could not only break the flocs and release extracellular materials, but also destroy cells and release intercellular materials from cells into the aqueous phase, and the degree of cell damage has a direct relationship with energy input [12,17,21]. Therefore, longer contact time inhibited microbial activity, which is not conducive to the subsequent AD and resulted in lower methane yield. Because the maximum CMP was observed at a CaO_2 dose of 0.1 g/gVSS and MW irradiation of 480 W/2 min (Table 1), this condition was selected for the pretreatment of WAS. In addition, several related conditions including sole CaO_2 (0.1 g/gVSS) oxidation or MW (480 W, 2 min) irradiation and the combination of these two processes with different sequences (CaO_2 (0.1 g/gVSS)/MW (480 W, 2 min) and MW (480 W, 2 min)/CaO_2 (0.1 g/gVSS)) were further investigated.


3.2 Improvement in sludge solubilization by pretreatment

The solubilization of WAS after pretreatment was evaluated using SCOD (Fig. 1 ). The values of SCOD in the tests of control, 0.1 gCaO_2/gVSS, and MW (480 W, 2 min) continued to increase within the first 4 d, while the increasing trend stopped in 3 d and 2 d in the MW (480 W, 2 min)/CaO_2 (0.1 g/gVSS) and CaO_2 (0.1 g/gVSS)/MW (480 W, 2 min) groups, respectively. The maximum value of SCOD accounted for 18%, 32%, 35%, 52%, and 54% of the TCOD in the control, 0.1 gCaO_2/gVSS, MW (480 W, 2 min), MW (480 W, 2 min)/CaO_2 (0.1 g/gVSS), and CaO_2 (0.1 g/gVSS)/MW (480 W, 2 min) groups, respectively. These findings indicate that the combination of CaO_2 and MW treatments can greatly enhance sludge disintegration and solubilization, and that adding CaO_2 before MW irradiation has a stronger effect than after MW irradiation. Reductions of VSS and TS were also measured on day 16 (Table 2 ). The highest reductions of VSS (55.6%) and TS (37.6%) were observed in the CaO_2 (0.1 g/gVSS)/MW (480 W, 2 min) group, which was consistent with the solubilization of WAS (Fig. 1).

When compared with the control test, pretreatment with 0.1 g CaO_2/gVSS, MW (480 W, 2 min), MW (480 W, 2 min)/CaO_2 (0.1 g/gVSS), and CaO_2 (0.1 g/gVSS)/MW (480 W, 2 min) resulted in improvements in the average values of solubilization in the initial 5 d of AD of 11.7%, 15.8%, 34.0%, and 36.8%, respectively (Fig. S1). The MW (480 W, 2 min)/CaO_2 (0.1 g/gVSS) and CaO_2 (0.1 g/gVSS)/MW (480 W, 2 min) treatments showed a synergistic effect leading to an additional 6.5% and 9.3% improvement in solubilization, respectively, relative to the sum of individual CaO_2 and MW pretreatments. Moreover, an average improvement in solubility of 2.8% was achieved by the CaO_2 (0.1 g/gVSS)/MW (480 W, 2 min) treatment sequence relative to the MW (480 W, 2 min)/CaO_2 (0.1 g/gVSS) treatment sequence. These results indicate that synergistic effects were achieved in the combined treatment systems, and the effects were greater in the CaO_2 (0.1 g/gVSS)/MW (480 W, 2 min) sequence.

During the CaO_2 and MW combination treatments, the WAS floc structure might be destroyed by several effects. MW treatment is capable of disintegrating the complex and breaking recalcitrant compounds down to simpler ones that can be more readily biodegradable [19], which is caused by both thermal and athermal effects. Thermal effects mainly occur via denaturation of cell materials, while athermal effects include electroporation, dielectric cell membrane rupture, magnetic field coupling, and selective heating [12]. CaO_2 destroys the sludge flocs and cell walls, mainly via the production of Ca(OH)_2 and H_2O_2 [25]. Since different mechanisms are applied in the CaO_2 and MW treatments to enhance sludge solubilization and the structure of the cell walls becomes more vulnerable to an attack after being weakened by another attack, the combination of these two processes can synergistically improve the disintegration and solubilization of sludge.

Because CaO_2 can decompose to Ca(OH)_2 and H_2O_2, the synergistic effects of CaO_2 and MW treatments can also be explained by the synergistic effects of Ca(OH)_2/MW and H_2O_2/MW. As shown in Table 3 , during 2 d of AD, the sludge solubilization was improved by 45.4% in the MW/Ca(OH)_2 treatment test and 45.8% in the Ca(OH)_2/MW treatment test, which was 7.4% and 7.8% higher than the sum of the individual Ca(OH)_2 and MW treatments, respectively. In the MW/H_2O_2 and H_2O_2/MW treatment tests, the sludge solubilization levels were improved by 22.6% and 25.2%, and they were 0.3% and 2.9% higher than the sum of individual H_2O_2 and MW treatments, respectively. Moreover, H_2O_2/MW led to 2.6% more SCOD over MW/H_2O_2 treatment, while there was no obvious difference between MW/Ca(OH)_2 and Ca(OH)_2/MW treatments. It has been reported that the synergistic effects of H_2O_2/MW were mainly ascribed to MW facilitating increased ^OH formation through the decomposition of H_2O_2, thus enhancing the oxidation and particulate COD disintegration of sludge [10,21]. Li et al. [25] indicated that ^OH is the main reactive oxygen species (ROS) in the CaO_2 oxidation system; therefore, ^OH formation in the tests with only CaO_2 and combined CaO_2/MW was also quantified. The reduction rates of the spin trap substance (p-CBA) were 18.3% and 26.5% in the 0.1 g CaO_2/gVSS and CaO_2 (0.1 g/g VSS)/MW (480 W, 2 min) treatments, respectively (Table 4 ). These findings indicate that the ^OH concentration in 0.1 g CaO_2/gVSS treatment was lower than in the CaO_2 (0.1 g/g VSS)/MW (480 W, 2 min) treatment. Moreover, the p-CBA reduction rates in the control and MW (480 W, 2 min) treatments were 5.7% and 6.8%, respectively. Therefore, the influence of MW irradiation on p-CBA reduction can be neglected, but MW irradiation facilitated the ^OH formation from CaO_2. ^OH is a powerful oxidizing agent that can react with most organic substrates and facilitate the dissolution of sludge, which is likely the main reason the synergistic effect of the CaO_2/MW treatment sequence was larger than that of the MW/CaO_2 sequence.

Although the SCOD was slightly higher in the combination treatment with Ca(OH)_2 and MW than in the combination treatment with CaO_2 and MW (Table 3), the pH was 10.8 in the former system and 8.2 in the later system after pretreatment. Thus, the Ca(OH)_2/MW treatment did not favor the methane production in the subsequent AD process. In the CaO_2/MW treatment system, CaO_2 release Ca(OH)_2 and H_2O_2 slowly; therefore, the pH increase in the beginning was not significant, and it slowly decreased to the neutral level (Fig. S2) during the AD process. Therefore, the methane production was not influenced remarkably.


3.3 Production of SCFAs during anaerobic digestion

In the control and MW (480 W, 2 min) tests, the concentration of TSCFAs increased continually during the first 6 and 8 days, respectively, then declined slowly because of the consumption by methanogens (Fig. 2 ). The TSCFAs concentration in the combined systems increased rapidly within the first 4 d, then declined because of methanogenesis. However, in tests with 0.1 gCaO_2/gVSS, there was an obvious increase within the first 4 d, after which the TSCFAs remained relatively stable due to the inhibition of methanogens [25]. These findings indicate that the addition of CaO_2 led to an obvious significant increase in TSCFAs production. This is because CaO_2 can facilitate the disintegration of WAS and improve the activities of both hydrolytic enzymes and acid-forming enzymes [25]. Variations in individual SCFAs during AD with different pretreatments are presented in Fig. S3. Acetic and propionic acids were the main species in all of the tests. The average percentages of acetic acid in the TSCFAs were 44.4%, 43.0%, 57.5%, 57.2% and 55.9%, respectively, in the control, MW (480 W, 2 min), 0.1 gCaO_2/gVSS, MW (480 W, 2 min)/CaO_2 (0.1 g/gVSS), and CaO_2 (0.1 g/gVSS)/MW (480 W, 2 min) treatments. Therefore, CaO_2 addition facilitated the conversion of other SCFAs to acetic acid, which is consistent with the results of our previous study [25].


3.4 Methane production during anaerobic digestion

The accumulation yields of CH_4 in all tests with pretreatment were lower than in the control test within the first 6 days (Fig. 3 ). This may be attributed to the potential inhibitory effects of MW and CaO_2 on microbial growth and activity. In our previous study, we found that the alkaline and oxidative conditions formed by CaO_2 inhibited the activity of methanogens [25]. As shown in Fig. S2, the pH value was 8.2 at day 0.5 in tests with the addition of 0.1 gCaO_2/gVSS, which is beyond the optimal pH range for methanogens. For MW, a previous study suggested that heat (high temperature) can inhibit methanogenesis [37]. However, after 6 days, the CH_4 production rate in tests with MW irradiation exceeded the control. There are two possible explanations for this phenomenon. First, the pH value in MW (480 W, 2 min)/CaO_2 (0.1 g/gVSS) and CaO_2 (0.1 g/gVSS)/MW (480 W, 2 min) tests decreased gradually after 0.5 d and was close to 7 at day 6, alleviating the inhibition of methanogens. Second, a relatively low temperature (51.4 ± 1.4 °C) was achieved after MW irradiation in this study, which was suitable for the later recovery of methanogenic activity. This will be further discussed from the viewpoint of microbial activity in Section 3.5.

Ultimate methane yields (mL per gram sample added) were used to evaluate the anaerobic biodegradability of WAS with different pretreatments. After 16 d of AD, the methane accumulation yield was 19.7 mL/gVSS in the control, while it increased by 23.9%, 70.1%, and 80.2%, respectively, in response to the MW (480 W, 2 min), MW (480 W, 2 min)/CaO_2 (0.1 g/gVSS), and CaO_2 (0.1 g/gVSS)/MW (480 W, 2 min) pretreatments (Fig. 3). Nevertheless, in 0.1 g CaO_2/gVSS tests, the cumulative methane production (10.4 mL/g VSS) was lower than those of the control tests because of the inhibition effect of CaO_2 on methanogens. It is obvious that proper MW irradiation not only alleviated these inhibition effects significantly, but also greatly improved methanogenesis after the microbial activity resumed. The observed increases in methane production in MW (480 W, 2 min)/CaO_2 (0.1 g/gVSS) and CaO_2 (0.1 g/gVSS)/MW (480 W, 2 min) treatments were much higher when compared with the values reported in other investigations of MW pretreatment. Eskicioglu et al. [38,39] indicated that MW irradiation (96–175 °C) treatment produced 16–31% higher biogas than the control after 15–18 d of mesophilic digestion. Rani et al. [15] drew the conclusion that the biogas production under the best microwave pretreatment was 35% higher than the control. Eskicioglu et al. [10] investigated MW irradiation (100 °C) combined with H_2O_2 (1 g/g TS) treatment and achieved a significant enhancement in methane yield that was 29 ± 3% higher than that of the control sample after 32 d of mesophilic digestion. The combination of MW and alkaline pretreatment was also investigated and 16–27% improvement of biogas production over the control were achieved [11,32,40]. The increases in methane production in the combined MW and CaO_2 treatment were also significant compared with other similar pretreatment technologies. When nitrous acid combined heat pre-treatment (35–70 °C) were used to enhance methane production from WAS, the methane potential of the WAS increased by 17–26% compared to the control [41]. It was improved by 10% at a digestion time of 16 d after incorporating the indigenous iron activated peroxidation pre-treatment [42]. Even though the presence of zero valent scrap iron (1.0 g/g VSS) could make methane production increased by 38.3% [43], it is still much less than the increase in this study. Therefore, CaO_2 combined with MW pretreatment can be a promising method for energy recovery from WAS.


3.5 Effect of CaO 2 /MW pretreatment on microbial activity and methanogenic archaea community


3.5.1 Microbial activity

Microorganisms play important roles during AD of WAS because large amounts of bacterial and archaeal populations are involved in sludge hydrolysis, acidification, and methanogenesis. Measurement of enzyme activities is an alternative method to assess microbial activity [25,44]. Proteins and carbohydrates make up the largest organic components of sewage sludge, accounting for approximately 53.6% and 11.0% of the total, respectively (Section 2.2). Thus, although a large number of enzymes might be involved in methane production during sludge AD, only those related to the degradation of proteins and carbohydrates were assayed.

Protease and α-amylase are responsible for the hydrolysis of proteins and carbohydrates, respectively. As shown in Fig. 4 (a), the activity of protease in the control was 35 μmol/min⋅gVSS on day 0.5, then decreased to 20 μmol/min⋅gVSS on day 1 and to below 10 μmol/min⋅gVSS over the next 7 days. In tests with CaO_2 (0.1 g/gVSS)/MW (480 W, 2 min) pretreatment, the activity of protease was 50 μmol/min⋅gVSS on day 0.5, then sharply decreased to 10 μmol/min⋅gVSS on day 1 and remained below 10 μmol/min⋅gVSS for the next 7 days. The activity of α-amylase in the control was 32.4 μmol/min⋅gVSS on day 0.5, then decreased to 24.3 and 20.4 μmol/min⋅gVSS on day 1 and 2, respectively, and remained below 20 μmol/min⋅gVSS over the next 6 days. In the test with CaO_2 (0.1 g/gVSS)/MW (480 W, 2 min) pretreatment, the activity of α-amylase was 53, 20 and 12.6 μmol/min⋅gVSS at 0.5, 1 and 2 d, respectively (Fig. 4(b)). These results indicate that the activities of protease and α-amylase in the tests with CaO_2 (0.1 g/gVSS)/MW (480 W, 2 min) were higher than in the control test at the beginning of AD. This is because more hydrolytic enzymes in the EPS were released into the supernatant due to solubilization of EPS after pretreatment, and they had a greater opportunity to contact substrates [45]. Moreover, the rate of decrease of hydrolytic enzyme activities was higher in the CaO_2 (0.1 g/gVSS)/MW (480 W, 2 min) treatment than in the control test during the first 2 days. These findings indicate that hydrolysis of proteins and carbohydrates occurred more quickly with the combined pretreatment.

PTA and AK are responsible for the formations of acetic acid [46]. The activities of PTA and AK presented a gradual increase in both the control and CaO_2 (0.1 g/gVSS)/MW (480 W, 2 min) tests within the initial 4 days, and the ascent rate was much higher in the CaO_2 (0.1 g/gVSS)/MW (480 W, 2 min) test (Fig. 4(c)). These findings indicate that acidification was greatly enhanced during the AD process with the combined pretreatment.

The activity of coenzyme F_420 (the key enzyme of methanogenesis) increased steadily within 8 days in the CaO_2 (0.1 g/gVSS)/MW (480 W, 2 min) group, while it increased in the initial 4 days and then decreased in the control (Fig. 4(d)). However, the activity of coenzyme F_420 in the test with CaO_2 (0.1 g/gVSS)/MW (480 W, 2 min) was lower than in the control test in the first 4 days. It is well known that methanogens are more sensitive to environmental change. Li et al. [25] found that the activity of coenzyme F_420 was suppressed by CaO_2 addition because of the alkaline and oxidative conditions formed by CaO_2. Moreover, the microwave irradiation process was found to limit microbial activity [47]. Therefore, pretreatment with the combined CaO_2 and MW did not favor the initial activity of methanogens. Nevertheless, coenzyme F_420 activity exceeded that of the control in 6 days when the AD process was employed. These findings imply that the methanogenic archaea were more active in the CaO_2 (0.1 g/gVSS)/MW (480 W, 2 min) test than the control test once they adapted to the environment.


3.5.2 Methanogenic archaea community structure

Methane production is closely related to the methanogenic archaea community structure. Therefore, the underlying mechanism of much higher methane production in the CaO_2 (0.1 g/gVSS)/MW (480 W, 2 min) test was investigated by analyzing the key functional archaeal population.

CaO_2 (0.1 g/gVSS)/MW (480 W, 2 min) treatment affected archaeal methanogen diversity, which can be clearly visualized in Fig. 5 . The Shannon diversities of methanogenic archaea sequences of samples from the control and CaO_2 (0.1 g/gVSS)/MW (480 W, 2 min) tests after 8 days were 5.3 and 4.5, respectively. These findings indicate that pretreatment with CaO_2 (0.1 g/gVSS)/MW (480 W, 2 min) decreased the archaeal diversity. Our previous study also indicated that the addition of CaO_2 alone decreased the number of methanogenic archaea [25]. Overall, it seems that although MW can improve the production of methane, methanogenic archaea diversity did not increase.

Fig. 6 illustrates the genus level distribution of the archaeal population in the control and CaO_2 (0.1 g/gVSS)/MW (480 W, 2 min) tests. In the CaO_2 (0.1 g/gVSS)/MW (480 W, 2 min) treatment, Methanospirillum sp., Methanosaeta sp., Methanobacteriu sp., Methanosarcina sp., Methanobrevibacter sp., and Methanosphaera sp. accounted for 44.2%, 30.7%, 9.6%, 1.6%, 1.3%, and 0.13% of the total archaeal sequences, respectively, while in the control test, their relative abundances were 29.9%, 26.8%, 6.8%, 2.4%, 0.7% and 0.07%, respectively. Obviously, the abundances of Methanospirillum sp. and Methanosaeta sp. improved in response to CaO_2/MW pretreatment. It has been reported that the cell surface structure of methanogens is diverse, and can be roughly divided into the pseudomurein, surface layer, methanochondroitin, and sheath [48]. Methanospirillum sp. and Methanosaeta sp. probably have the most complex cell envelopes of any archaea and exist as filamentous chains that are enclosed by a unique tubular paracrystalline proteinaceous sheath. This sheath exhibits a very low porosity and has strong recurrent covalent links containing cysteine, which results in an unusually stable layer that resists dissociation by conventional treatments [48,49]. This is likely the main reason for the predominance of Methanospirillum sp. and Methanosaeta sp. under the CaO_2/MW pretreatment condition.

Methanogenic microorganisms are distinguished by two groups: one that mainly uses acetic acid for methane production and another that primarily uses H_2/CO_2 [50–52]. Thus, acetic acid concentration has a significant impact on the composition of methanogen communities. The average percentage of acetic acid in TSCFAs with the CaO_2 (0.1 g/gVSS)/MW (480 W, 2 min) pretreatment (55.9%) was higher than that in the control test (44.4%) (Fig. S3). The higher level of acetic acid could benefit the growth of acetate-utilizing methanogens. Methanosaeta sp. and Methanosarcina sp. are obligate acetate-utilizing methanogens [50,52,53]; therefore, their percentages were higher in the CaO_2 (0.1 g/gVSS)/MW (480 W, 2 min) group than the control. In the AD process, H_2 is produced in the acidogenesis process and CO_2 is generated in both the acetogenesis and aceticlastic methanogenesis processes. Therefore, the yields of H_2 and CO_2 in the CaO_2 (0.1 g/gVSS)/MW (480 W, 2 min) group should also be higher than in the control due to the strengthened AD process. To determine if the endogenous H_2 could be utilized by hydrogenotrophic methanogens to generate CH_4 by fixing CO_2, the concentrations of H_2 and CO_2 in the biogas were tested. The results showed that the CO_2 concentration in the CaO_2 (0.1 g/gVSS)/MW (480 W, 2 min) test was lower than in the control, and it should be noted that H_2 accumulation did not occur (Table S1). When combined with the abundant hydrogenotrophic methanogens (such as Methanospirillum sp.) in the CaO_2 (0.1 g/gVSS)/MW (480 W, 2 min) groups, it can be inferred that almost all of the produced H_2 was utilized and resulted in a substantial decrease of CO_2 and a gradual increase of CH_4 in the biogas. These findings were confirmed by the high methane percentage (70.0%) in the biogas in the CaO_2 (0.1 g/gVSS)/MW (480 W, 2 min) group relative to that of the control (52.2%; Table S1). Hu et al. [54] reported that hydrogenotrophic methanogenesis is a very fast H_2-consuming process that helps maintain a low H_2 pressure in AD for successful operation. Thus, it makes sense that the pathway of hydrogenotrophic methanogenesis also improved following pretreatment with CaO_2 (0.1 g/gVSS)/MW (480 W, 2 min). Kim et al. [55] obtained 49% methane in WAS samples (VS/TS = 75%) after 50 d of AD at 35 °C, which increased to 63.4% and 58.8% after heat pretreatment at 180 °C and 210 °C, respectively. When compared with their study, the level of methane in biogas was greatly improved by CaO_2/MW pretreatment in the present study, indicating that this method is an effective technology for WAS treatment and energy recovery.


3.6 Dewaterability of waste activated sludge

Sludge dewatering is of paramount importance in wastewater treatment systems because it reduces sludge volume and, consequently, the cost of transporting sludge to its ultimate disposal site. Moreover, dewatered sludge is generally much easier to handle. The dewaterability of sludge is related to the distribution of water in the sludge, while the latter is closely related to the physical structure of the sludge [56]. The physical structure of sludge has been shown to be altered by pretreatment [57]. The effects of different pretreatments used in this study on the dewatering characteristics of sludge are summarized in Table 5 . In the control, the CST and SRF values were 42.1 s and 15.7 × 10^12 m/kg, respectively, which increased to 82.6 s and 22 × 10^12 m/kg in the 0.1 gCaO_2/gVSS treatment, indicating that the addition of CaO_2 deteriorated sludge dewaterability. Erden [32] and Neyens et al. [58] reported that treating WAS with alkali deteriorated the dewaterability of WAS. This degeneration likely resulted from the breakdown of sludge and increase of fine particles [59]. The CST and SRF values in the MW (480 W, 2 min) treatment test decreased to 28.7 s and 9.2 × 10^12 m/kg, respectively, indicating that MW (480 W, 2 min) treatment improved the filterability of sludge. The best dewaterability of sludge was obtained in the combined pretreatment application because the CST and SRF values decreased significantly relative to the control sludge. Erden [32] and Yu et al. [57] indicated that MW pretreatment led to improvement of the dewatering performance of sludge. Guo et al. [60] illustrated that microwave digested sludge showed a reduction of CST by 11.1% and 10.7%, relative to the control and conventionally heated digested sludge. Nalan and Isil [61] reported that MW/H_2O_2 provided two-fold higher improvement in sludge dewaterability. Suitable microwave irradiation reduced the amount of ultra colloid in sludge and enhanced sludge dewaterability [57]. Therefore, although CaO_2 could hinder the sludge dewaterability, its combination with MW still greatly improved sludge dewaterability.


4 Conclusions

This study demonstrates that combined CaO_2 and MW treatment effectively improved the solubilization and biodegradability of WAS. The main conclusions are as follows:

Combined CaO_2 and MW pretreatment synergistically enhanced solubilization of the WAS and methane production during subsequent anaerobic digestion. The optimal pretreatment conditions were identified as addition of CaO_2 (0.1 g/gVSS) followed by MW irradiation (480 W, 2 min). Under this pretreatment condition, the methane accumulation yield after 16 d of anaerobic digestion increased by 80.2% relative to the control.

The synergistic effects of the combined CaO_2/MW pretreatment originated from different mechanisms applied in the CaO_2 (alkaline and oxidative condition) and MW (thermal and athermal effects) treatments. MW irradiation also facilitated increased ^OH formation from CaO_2.

CaO_2 (0.1 g/gVSS)/MW (480 W, 2 min) pretreatment promoted the growth of hydrogenotrophic methanogens (Methanospirillum sp.) and acetate-utilizing methanogens (Methanosaeta sp.). Thus, the overall methane yield increased significantly relative to the control.

The dewaterability of WAS after anaerobic digestion improved considerably following combined CaO_2/MW pretreatment.
