TITLE: Thermophilic anaerobic digestion of raw sugarcane vinasse


1 Introduction

At the end of the 2013/2014 Brazilian sugarcane harvesting season, approximately 27 billion liters of ethanol was produced. Of this amount, 10.8% was sold to external markets, leaving the majority (89.22%) to supply the domestic market [1]. However, in spite of the contribution of ethanol production to the Brazilian economy, the development of this sector has not been followed by environmental control measures, especially with respect to the most abundant effluent, the vinasse or stillage, originating from the distillation of ethanol.

Generally, the vinasse is applied directly to soil because of its organic matter and nutrient content (especially potassium, but also nitrogen and phosphorus), which makes it a good organic fertilizer for sugarcane culture regardless of whether it is used at ideal proportions. Through an economic perspective, the soil application of vinasse represents the simplest and cheapest solution. Furthermore, soil application of this residue is supported by a statewide technical norm decreed in São Paulo State, in which the main restriction is potassium content (400 kg-K_2O.ha^−1.year^−1) [2,3].

However, a major problem in using vinasse is that the large volume (12–15 L of residue for every liter of ethanol produced) triggers a range of environmental effects in the planted area when continuous application of vinasse to the soil is made. The environmental impacts include the following: (i) soil and groundwater contamination [4,5], (ii) leaching and salinization [6–8], (iii) seed germination inhibition [9,10] and (iv) the proliferation of the “cowshed-fly” (Stomoxys calcitrans). This last effect is a recent and local problem that changes the behavior of bovine and equine animals, including reductions in weight gain and milk production. To avoid these environmental effects, changes in the statewide technical norm in São Paulo State are required.

Anaerobic digestion is a viable option for sugarcane vinasse processing and enables energy recovery as hydrogen and methane production using a two-stage acidogenic and methanogenic system without interfering in its quality as bio-fertilizer.

In addition to conventional anaerobic digestion from a combustible biogas (CH_4), a previous study has successfully demonstrated that up-flow anaerobic packed-bed reactors (APBR) can be successfully used for continuous thermophilic hydrogen production using raw sugarcane vinasse as a substrate [11]. In this previous study, an optimal organic loading rate (OLR) of 84.2 kg-COD m^−3 d^−1 was determined, resulting in maximum values of hydrogen production and yield of 5252.6 NmL-H_2.d^−1 and 3.7 mol-H_2.mol^−1 _total carbohydrates, respectively.

In terms of methane production, two main studies have demonstrated the feasibility of sugarcane vinasse anaerobic digestion. A pilot-scale (75 m^3) thermophilic (55 °C) up-flow anaerobic sludge blanket reactor (UASB) plant has been in operation in Brazil from the mid-1980s and is able to treat sugarcane vinasse at an organic loading rate (OLR) of 26.5 kg-COD m^−3 d^−1 with COD removal above 70% and a methane yield of 310 NmL-CH_4.g^−1COD_removed. However, these data correspond to the average values for the last 50 days of UASB operation [12]. To the best of our knowledge, this study represents the only reference focused on the anaerobic digestion of Brazilian sugarcane vinasse. A later study by Harada et al. [13] reported the thermophilic (55 °C) anaerobic treatment of cane molasses vinasse using a UASB (volume of 140 l) for 430 days in Thailand. The authors observed a COD removal of 60% and due to operational issues, an estimated methane yield of 290 NmL-CH_4.g^−1COD_removed was reported.

As exposed, UASB reactors were used in both studies, very likely for being a consolidated technology with various systems operating in full scale worldwide while, thermophilic digestion remains a logical treatment option for sugarcane vinasse and leaves the distillation columns with temperatures between 85 °C and 90 °C. Furthermore, sludge grown using a thermophilic process possesses an intrinsically higher methanogenic activity than mesophilic sludge [14].

However, the increase in the value of methane yield in the methanogenic phase has been questioned because the separation of the acidogenic phase, which was first suggested to enhance process control, inhibits the hydrogen to methane conversion pathway [15]. This concept disagrees with the findings of Luo et al. [16] who observed an enhancement of 11% in the overall energy efficiency of a two-stage system compared with a single stage system when treating cassava stillage at 55 °C and the observations of Nasr et al. [17] who observed an increase of 18.5% in the two-stage process compared with the single-stage process based on the biogas energy value (methane and hydrogen) produced in each system from thin stillage at 37 °C.

Other studies have reported the simultaneous production of hydrogen and methane using a two-stage process; however, the comparisons of the system performance and overall energy efficiency in relation to the single-stage process were not covered (Table 1 ). Therefore, the aim of this research was to compare the performance of a single-stage methanogenic UASB reactor (UASB I) with a two-stage (APBR-UASB II) system using thermophilic conditions and to determine whether a significant difference exists in the potential energy yields between these systems.


2 Methods

Hydrogen production has been previously studied in an anaerobic packed-bed reactor (APBR) that was inoculated using the natural wastewater fermentation process [23]. The pH of the vinasse was adjusted to 6.5 adding 50% (w/v) NaOH solution. The APBR was operated with an OLR of 84.2 kg-COD m^−3 d^−1 and a hydraulic retention time (HRT) of 10.2 h at 55 °C. No pH control was used along the packed-bed reactor. More details regarding the APBR configuration, support material, and inoculation procedure as well as the organization and behavior of the microbial community based on next-generation sequencing ((NGS) 454-pyrosequencing) have been previously reported [11].

In this study, the methane production experiments were performed in up-flow sludge blanket reactors (UASB) running in single-stage (UASB I) and two-stage system (UASB II). For this purpose raw sugarcane vinasse and acidified sugarcane vinasse were used. This last was provided from an APBR running in parallel with operational conditions optimized to the hydrogen production, as aforementioned.


2.1 Methanogenic reactors

UASB I was built with two acrylic sheets opposite (front and back) a rectangular section (L_1 – length – 780 mm; L_2 – width – 30 mm; and L_3 – thickness – 100 mm). The reactor sidebands (L_1 – length −780 mm; and L_4 - width – 120 mm) for closing the acrylic sheets were made of stainless steel. The feed zone (L_5 – length – 50 mm) was equipped with a perforated stainless plate for uniform distribution of the influent. The main body of the reactor for the development of the sludge blanket had a rectangular prism shape (L_6 – length – 450 mm). The upper part of the reactor just above the main body was 120 mm long (L_7). This zone of the reactor sustained the three-phase separator, which is the system responsible for the separation of the liquid effluent, biogas and suspended biomass. The liquid volume of UASB I was 10 L (Fig. 1 A and B).

UASB II was built using acrylic tubes (internal diameter – 80 mm; outer diameter – 88 mm). The main body of the reactor for the development of the sludge blanket was 440 mm long (L_1). The upper part of the reactor just above the main body (internal diameter – 110 mm; outer diameter – 118 mm) was 150 mm long (L_2). This zone of the reactor sustained the three-phase separator, which is the system responsible for the separation of the liquid effluent, biogas and suspended biomass. The liquid volume of UASB II was 3.4 L (Fig. 1C).


2.1.1 Sugarcane vinasse

Raw sugarcane vinasse was used as the substrate to assess its hydrogen production potential. Before entering into single-stage methane production (UASB I) and the first-stage hydrogen production in an APBR, the raw sugarcane vinasse was filtered with a paper filter (Nalgon, density of 80 g m^2, porosity of 3 μm) to reduce the concentration of the suspended solids. The chemical composition of the raw vinasse was previously determined and is as follows: total carbohydrates: 4.1 ± 0.9 g L^−1; chemical oxygen demand – COD_Filtered (3 μm): 35.2 ± 2.6 g L^−1; and soluble COD_(0.45 μm): 25.8 ± 4.5 g L^−1. A detailed raw sugarcane vinasse composition was previously published [11]. The acidified sugarcane vinasse used as a substrate for the UASB II had the following composition: total carbohydrates: 0.94 ± 0.01 g L^−1; COD_Filtered (3 μm): 24.0 ± 1.8 g L^−1; and SCOD_(0.45 μm): 20.9 ± 1.0 g L^−1. The COD values for each substrate (raw and acidified vinasse) were used as a parameter for the OLR calculation.


2.1.2 Inoculation

UASB reactors were inoculated to a third of their total volume with an adapted anaerobic sludge from a sugarcane vinasse treatment plant operating at 55 °C (São Martinho factory, Pradopólis, Sao Paulo, Brazil).


2.1.3 Experimental procedures

The methane production was evaluated in UASB reactors running in single (UASB I) and two-stage system (UASB II). The reactors were fed continuously with raw and acidified sugarcane vinasse, respectively, by a peristaltic pump (Gilson Minipuls). The operation of both methanogenic reactors was divided into four phases (Table 2 ). In the first 27 days (Phase I), the reactors were fed with vinasse with no pH adjustment and at OLR of 15 kg-COD m^−3 d^−1 and 55 °C. The partial alkalinity of the medium was not able to maintain the reactors buffered at Phase I; therefore, the initial OLR of 15 kg-COD m^−3 d^−1 was maintained and an external source of buffering was added. A sodium bicarbonate (NaHCO_3) solution at concentrations of 12.5 g L^−1 and 6.25 g L^−1 were added to the influent of UASB I and II, respectively (Phase II). These concentrations were the lowest concentrations capable of increasing the influent pH to 6.8–7.2 (optimum pH for methanogenic reactors). Due to this operational problem, Phases I and II were considered the acclimation phase. However, in Phases III and IV, the OLR was increased to 20 kg-COD m^−3 d^−1 and 25 kg-COD m^−3 d^−1, respectively.


2.1.4 Analytical methods

To evaluate the performance of the reactors, samples from the influent and effluent were taken periodically, and the total carbohydrate concentration [24], pH, total chemical oxygen demand (TCOD), soluble chemical oxygen demand (SCOD) and volatile suspended solids (VSS) were analyzed [25].

Organic acid (acetic, propionic, isobutyric, butyric, isovaleric, valeric and caproic) and solvent (methanol, ethanol and n-butanol) concentrations were analyzed using gas chromatography (Shimadzu GC 2010) equipped with an FID detector as described in Perna et al. [26].

The gas volume was measured using a Ritter^® MilligasCounter. The analytical composition of the biogas, which consisted of hydrogen (H_2), carbon dioxide (CO_2), and methane (CH_4), was determined using a Shimadzu gas chromatograph-2010^® GC equipped with a capillary column Carboxen 1010 PLOT (30 m × 0.32 mm) and thermal conductivity detector [27]. The gas volumes were corrected to standard temperature (0 °C) and pressure (1 atm) (STP).

3 Results and discussion


3.1 COD removal in UASB I and APBR-UASB II

The methanogenic reactors were operated for 180 days. The startup of UASB II took into account the APBR operational stability in which the variation coefficient was 7.5% based on the conversion of COD, a variable adopted for the OLR calculation of both reactors (Fig. 2 A). During Phase I, the effluent pH from UASB I and UASB II was 5.7 ± 0.3 and 6.5 ± 0.3, respectively. After NaHCO_3 addition to the influent, the values of the effluent pH ranged between 8.3 and 8.5 (Fig. 2B). Moreover, influent buffering recovered the reactors, resulting in increased removal of total and soluble COD (Fig. 2C).

Total and soluble COD removal increased with an increase in the OLR (15–25 kg-COD m−^3 d^−1), reaching values equal to 60.7% ± 0.3 and 72.6 ± 1.2% (UASB I) and 63.0% ± 1.5 and 72.6 ± 1.7% (UASB II), respectively, at the end of the operation of the reactors (Fig. 2D and E).

Regarding APBR-UASB II, the total and soluble COD removal was 74.6% ± 0.3 and 96.1 ± 1.7%, respectively, at the end of the operation of the reactor (i.e., at an OLR of 25 kg-COD m^−3 d^−1). These values are similar to the values reported by Souza et al. [12] and higher than the values observed for UASB I and those reported by Harada et al. [13]. Additionally, the carbohydrate concentration effluent values were less than 15 mg L^−1 (minimum value detectable using the analytical method), indicating that the carbohydrate removal for both reactors was higher than 98.5%.


3.2 Methane production in UASB I and APBR-UASB II and COD mass balance

The OLR increase by reducing the HRT of the UASB reactors and the addition of NaHCO_3 to the influent of the reactors had a strong influence on methane production (Fig. 3 , Tables 2 and 3 ). After influent buffering, the methane content of the biogas increased from approximately 17% (both reactors) to 61% (UASB I) and 68.8% (UASB II). Methane production and methane yield increased with the increase of the OLR (15–25 kg-COD m^−3 d^−1) as presented in Fig. 3A–D. Furthermore, volumetric methane production (VMP) was higher in UASB II, indicating that acidogenic phase separation increased the energy extraction, which was confirmed by examining the methane yield values of 234.2 NmL-CH_4.g^−1COD_removed and 306 NmL-CH_4 g^−1COD_removed at the end of the operation of UASB I and UASB II, respectively. Considering the maximum theoretical yield of methane (350 NmL-CH_4.g^−1COD), this value was 87.4% of the maximum theoretical yield of methane, which is similar to Souza et al. [12] and Harada et al. [13].

To identify the energy flow direction of the process and validate the reliability of the data, COD mass balance was performed for the methanogenic reactors (UASB I and UASB II). The products detected using analytical methods represented 84.7%–99.6% (Tables S1 and S2) of the total COD measured. From this range, approximately 31.5% was converted to methane, 36% was incorporated into the biomass, and only 7.8% was not detected.

Sugarcane vinasse is poorly biodegradable (BOD_5/COD ratio of 0.47); however, after the anaerobic digestion of sugarcane vinasse, the ABD _v values were 66% and 87.4% for the single (UASB I) and two-stage (APBR-UASB II) systems, respectively. The two-stage system resulted in a 21% ABD increase versus the single stage reactor. Additionally, the ABD _vincrease reflected in the energy extraction suggested that according to Equation (3), a liter of vinasse in UASB I generated 36.2 W, whereas a liter of vinasse in the APBR-UASB II generated 45.5 W, with 1.5 W corresponding to hydrogen production in the first stage [11] and 44.2 W corresponding to methane production in the second stage (APBR-UASB II). As a result, the energy extraction in the two-stage system was increased 25.7% compared with UASB I. This value is much higher than the values observed by Luo et al. [16] and Nasr et al. [17] and certainly indicates a requirement for the acidogenic phase to increase the ABD _v and energy extraction from sugarcane vinasse.


3.4 Evaluation of volatile fatty acids (VFA)

The minimization of the distribution of electrons and an increased bioenergy yield were observed in UASB I and UASB II from larger chain acids (butyric, valeric and caproic) converted to acetic acid, followed by acetic acid conversion to methane (final electron acceptor of anaerobic process) (Tables 4 and 5 ).

Acetate and propionic acids were the dominant species and contributed to 57–70% and 26–42%, respectively, of the total VFA in the effluent. Propionic acid degradation is known to be the rate-limiting step of overall methane production during thermophilic anaerobic digestion [31]. However, independent of the OLR increase, the effluent propionic acid concentrations were constant throughout the experiment for both UASB I and UASB II.

The soluble COD mass balance from Phase II for both reactors was close to 100%. We believe that the soluble compounds not analyzed (glycerol and melanoidins) were converted to VFA, which is supported by the low values (≈75%) for the soluble COD mass balance in Phase I, represented by system instability. However, even assuming these above-mentioned compounds were converted in VFAs, toxicity studies of treated sugarcane vinasse must be performed.


3.5 Final remarks

From an environmental perspective, the biodigested vinasse from the two-stage system (APBR-UASB II) might be used as biofertilizer in the sugarcane crop due to its lower pollutant load that is four times less than its initial organic matter concentration.

From an energy perspective, hydrogen production was not competitive compared with methane production. However, hydrogen in fuel cells is still an ideal energy source. In addition, the production of hydrogen is rapid and, as mentioned, the first phase of the two-stage system APBR-UASB II increased the energy extraction by 25.7% compared with UASB I. Currently, many challenges still must be overcome to increase the hydrogen production and yield. In contrast, the methane production values obtained in this research revealed that considerable energy gains for biorefineries (that are generally lost in the direct soil application by fertirrigation) may be achieved if the methane generated from the anaerobic digestion of vinasse is used for (i) electricity generation or (ii) diesel replacement. Another alternative use for methane would be (iii) cogeneration systems in which the energy also stimulates second-generation ethanol production due to the release of bagasse by combustion.

Finally, considering that sugarcane vinasse plants operate for only 200 useful days per year, studies that examine the successful re-start of the thermophilic plant, the role of microbial communities in the anaerobic digestion of sugarcane vinasse and the persistence of these communities during the inter-harvest period in the reactors are essential to enable full-scale application of these process.


4 Conclusions

Based on the findings of this study, the following conclusions can be drawn:• The two-stage system achieved better performance compared with the single-stage system for the treatment of sugarcane vinasse under thermophilic conditions. In addition to the two-stage system, the total and soluble COD removal were 74.6% ± 0.3 and 96.1 ± 1.7%, respectively, at an OLR of 25 kg-COD m^−3 d^−1. • A methane yield of 306 NmL-CH_4.g-COD_removed was achieved in the two-stage process. • Compared with the single-stage system, the energy yield increased 25.7% using two-stage anaerobic digestion, indicating that the acidogenic phase separation did not prevent the hydrogen to methane pathway. • The effluent propionic acid concentrations were constant throughout the experiment for both reactors independent of the OLR increase.
