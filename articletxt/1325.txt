TITLE: Biorefinery for efficient xanthan gum, ethanol, and biogas production from potato crop residues


1 Introduction

Potato (Solanum tuberosum) is ranked third among food crops globally [1]. Every year, about 370 million tons of potato is produced throughout the world [2]. Since about 125 g potato crop residues (PCR), comprising stems, leaves and roots, remains per kg of harvested potato [3], about 46 million tons of PCR is generated per year, which is mainly unused. The critical factor that distinguishes PCR from other agricultural residues such as rice straw, wheat straw, and sugarcane bagasse is the presence of high amounts of toxic materials. The toxic glycoalkaloids, mainly α-solanine and α-chaconine, are present in all parts of the potato plant. The amounts of these materials in stems (23–33 mg per kg of fresh weight), leaves (230–1000 mg per kg of fresh weight), and roots (180–400 mg per kg of fresh weight) are very high [4]. Unlike sugarcane bagasse, wheat straw, and rice straw that can be used or converted to animal feed, potato stems, leaves and roots are very toxic materials for animals. The compositional analysis of potato crop residues shows the differences of this crop residue from others. For example, rice straw is composed of 38.7% cellulose, 28.7% hemicellulose, and 13.7% lignin [5], which is different from PCR compositions (48.2% glucan, 18.2% xylan and 27.6% lignin).

Notably, PCR, as a lignocellulosic material, contains cellulosic compounds which can be hydrolyzed to simple sugars (monomers) and subsequently converted to valuable biofuels and biopolymers by microorganisms [6]. Nonetheless, the recalcitrance structure of lignocellulosic materials is the main bottleneck. Accordingly, destruction of this structural barrier to facilitate the contact of hydrolyzing enzymes with cellulose is necessitated [7,8]. In this regard, different pretreatment methods, including biological, physical, chemical methods or their combination, have been proposed [9]. Being a chemical pretreatment, organosolv has provided efficient pathways resulting in lignin, an easily bioconvertible cellulose, and a solution of hemicellulose sugars [10]. This technique has been studied using an acid, a base or a catalyst [11].

Different organic solvents have been implemented for the pretreatment of lignocellulosic biomass, like methanol, ethanol, acetone, ethylene glycerol, and butanol [12]. Because of the unique properties of ethanol, i.e., low cost, lack of toxicity, ease of recovery and excellent miscibility with water, it is the most commonly proposed alcohol solvent in the organosolv pretreatments of lignocelluloses [13].

During the last decades, fossil fuels have been mainly used to provide the growing global energy demand. However, overconsumption of these fuels has contributed to the environmental problems of global warming, greenhouse-gasses emissions, and unexpected climate change [14,15]. To protect oil resources and preserve the global climate, scientific societies have paid increasing attention to sustainable bio-energies [16,17].

Bioethanol is an environmentally-friendly renewable substitute to gasoline that can be produced from biomass [18]. Because of its high oxygen content, the combustion of bioethanol is less polluting in comparison to petroleum fuels, thus helping to reduce toxic substance emissions [19]. Utilizing bioethanol rather than fossil-based gasoline also helps the reduction of carbon emission up to 80% [20]. Biogas, the most common gaseous biofuels, is a green fuel mainly produced during the anaerobic digestion of organic materials. Besides the production of fertilizers, anaerobic digestion has the ability to reduce pathogens, odor, and greenhouse-gas emissions [21,22]. Consequently, biogas and bioethanol are among the most attractive and eco-friendly alternatives to fossil fuels [23].

Xanthan gum, the most important microbial polysaccharide on an industrial scale, is a branched biopolymer mainly produced by a bacterium, i.e., Xanthomonas campestris [24,25]. Stability under alkali, acid, and heat conditions, emulsibility, suspensibility and thickening have led to the growing utilization of xanthan gum in large numbers of industries, including pharmaceutical, food, cosmetic and oil ones [16,26]. Nevertheless, the production cost is high due to the high cost of glucose and sucrose as the most utilized carbon resources for the large-scale microbial production of xanthan gum. Lignocellulosic materials have been introduced as a potential low-cost alternative resource to reduce fermentation costs [27].

Although conversion of PCR to biofuels and xanthan gum has not been investigated before, bioproduction of these products from other lignocelluloses has been reported earlier. For instance, bioconversion of wheat straw to bioethanol has been reported using ethanol pre-extraction step and subsequently, sodium carbonate pretreatment. The results showed that 6.5% wv^−1 ethanol could be obtained after ethanolic fermentation by Saccharomyces cerevisiae [28]. Moreover, the pretreatment of sweet sorghum stalk with ethanol, based on a biorefinery approach, provided a high yield of ethanol (72.5%) and biogas (278 mL g^−1 VS [29]. Additionally, alkali pretreatment of rice straw could improve the yield of xanthan gum five times, in comparison with the untreated rice straw [30]. PCR with high cellulose and hemicellulose content has a high potential for use as a nutrition source for the microbial production of xanthan gum; However, to our knowledge, there is no study in this regard.

This study, therefore, aimed to investigate the use of PCR as a nutrition source for the production of xanthan gum, ethanol, and biogas. Hence, PCR was subjected to ethanolic organosolv pretreatment. The solids recovered after pretreatment were enzymatically hydrolyzed while the remaining liquid was subjected to anaerobic digestion. The enzymatic hydrolysis resulted in residual solids and a supernatant (hydrolysate). Moreover, the residual solids were anaerobically digested. The supernatant was utilized as a source for the microbial production of xanthan gum (Fig. 1 a) or bioethanol production (Fig. 1b).


2 Materials and methods


2.1 Collection and preparation of PCR

Potato crop residues (PCR) were obtained from a potato farm located at Fereydun Shahr (32° 94′ 01″ N, 50° 12′ 40″ E, Isfahan, Iran) and harvested in October 2019. The residues used in this study consisted of stalks, leaves, and roots of the potato plants. However, the roots were lower than stalks and leaves. They were ball-milled and sieved through 20 and 80 mesh screens to collect relatively homogenized particles with the size of less than 1 mm. They were then dried at 105 °C overnight and stored in sealed plastic bags at room temperature for further use.


2.2 PCR characterization and pretreatment

To conduct the organosolv pretreatment, a pressure reactor (SS304, 650 mL total volume) equipped with a pressure gauge and a temperature sensor was used [31]. The substrate (20 g, dry weight) and 180 g of organosolv solution were mixed in the reactor. The pretreatments were conducted using different solvent concentrations (50 or 75% vv^−1 ethanol solution), in the presence or absence of the catalyst (1% ww^−1 H_2SO_4), at 120, 140 and 180 °C for 60 min.

The concentration of 1% sulfuric acid, as a catalyst, in ethanolic organosolv pretreatments was chosen according to the previous studies done on the pretreatment of different lignocellulosic materials like pine and elm woods [32,33], sorghum stalks [29], olive tree pruning biomass [34], and rice straw [35]. In these studies, it was clearly observed that the disruption of the recalcitrance structure of lignocellulosic feedstocks could be improved by the addition of 1% sulfuric acid, as a catalyst, in ethanolic organosolv pretreatments. Hence, in this work, the same strategy was implemented for the pretreatment of PCR.

The reaction mixture obtained at the end of pretreatment was filtered. The obtained filter cake (recovered solids) was washed with 120 mL of 50% vv^−1 ethanol solution and then rinsed with distilled water. Afterward, the recovered solids were dried in an oven at 40 °C for 2 days. Next, the dried recovered solids were weighed and kept at 4 °C until use. The filtrate (liquor) contained ethanol, which was recovered using a laboratory distillation apparatus. The remaining liquid obtained after the distillation stage was kept at −20 °C for further biogas production.

Solid recovery was defined as the weight of the recovered solids obtained after pretreatment per initial weight of the sample. It was calculated according to Eq. (1): FORMULA: Solid recovery = Recovered solids remaining after pretratment [ g ] Weight of the sample [ g ] × 100

Carbohydrates (Glucan, xylan, other sugars), ash and lignin contents of the recovered solids, as well as the untreated PCR, were determined based on the method introduced by the National Renewable Energy Laboratory [36].

Moreover, the samples were subjected to Scanning electron microscopy (SEM, Zeiss, Germany) to compare the morphological and structural changes that occurred during the pretreatments.


2.3 Enzymatic hydrolysis of the recovered solids

Enzymatic hydrolysis method used in this study had been developed before and implemented for the pretreatment of softwood pine [37], municipal solid waste [38] and safflower straw [22].

The hydrolyses of recovered solids were conducted in 118 mL sealable glass bottles (Pajouhesh Sepahan, Isfahan, Iran) using the mixture of Cellic® CTec2 and Cellic® HTec2, which was kindly provided by Novozymes (Bagsvaerd, Denmark). The activities of the two enzymes (Cellic® CTec2 and Cellic® HTec2) were determined as 125 and 10 filter paper units per mL (FPU mL^−1), respectively, according to the method presented by Adney and Baker [39]. Then, the enzymes were mixed according to the ratio of 1:9 (ratio of Cellic Htec2 to Cellic Ctech2); this mixture had an activity of 113.5 FPU mL^−1 [40]. The selection of the 1:9 ratio of two enzymes was based on the ratio suggested by the producer (Novozymes A/S · Luna No. 2010-01668-01) for the enzymatic hydrolysis of lignocellulosic biomass. Additionally, the reason for the application of Cellic Ctec2 was that it is a cellulase complex for the degradation of cellulose to fermentable sugars, i.e., it is a blend of aggressive cellulose with a high activity of β-glucosidases and hemicellulase. According to the enzymes producer's suggestion, the combination of Cellic Ctec2 and Htech2 boosts cellulose hydrolysis when the pretreated feedstock of interest contains an appreciable amount of hemicellulose. The compositional analysis of the raw PCR also indicated that it contained a considerable amount of xylose (18.2%), thus reflecting a high amount of hemicellulose. Therefore, to enhance the cellulose hydrolysis, Cellic Htech2 was added to Ctech2. Moreover, Htech2 is an enzyme converting hemicelluloses to fermentable sugars. Therefore, the presence of Cellic Htec2 in the enzyme mixture enhanced cellulose and hemicellulose hydrolysis. Each bottle was filled with 1 g substrate and 20 mL of sodium citrate buffer (50 mM, pH 4.8). For enzymatic hydrolysis, 20 FPU of the enzyme mixture per gram of the substrate (based on dry weight) was added to each bottle, as described previously [40].

The incubation of bottles was then carried out for 72 h at 45 ± 1 °C and 120 rpm (Jal Tajhiz, JSTDL 40, Iran). At the end of the hydrolysis, a sample was taken to measure the sugar concentration. The sugar concentration was measured using a high performance liquid chromatography (HPLC). The system was equipped with refractive index (RI) and ultraviolet–visible (UV/Vis) detectors (Jasco International Co., Tokyo, Japan). An ion-exchange column (Aminex HPX-87P, Bio-Rad Laboratories, Hercules, USA) was then implemented in the HPLC system. The temperature of the column was 80 °C. The mobile phase was deionized water at the flow rate of 0.6 mL min^−1.

The glucose yield was calculated according to Eq. (2) [41]. FORMULA: Glucose yield ( % ) = Glucose concentration ( g l ) Glucan concentration ( g l ) × 1.111 × 100

The factor 1.111 is the hydration factor, which is considered for the conversion of glucan to glucose.


2.4 Ethanol production

The hydrolysates obtained from enzymatic hydrolysis were used for the preparation of the medium. They were supplemented with (g L^−1): 7.5, (NH_4)_2SO_4; 5, yeast extract; 3.5, K_2HPO_4; 1, CaCl_2·2H_2O; and 0.75, MgSO_4·7H_2O. Subsequently, pH was set to 5 with the addition of the NaOH solution (2 M). After autoclavation, they were inoculated with 1 g L^−1 Saccharomyces cerevisiae (University of Gothenburg culture collection, CCUG 53310, Sweden). The anaerobic fermentation was performed at 32 ± 0.5 °C and 130 rpm for 48 h [42]. Liquid samples were taken to measure the concentration of ethanol. Ethanol concentration was measured using HPLC (Jasco International Co., Tokyo, Japan). An Aminex HPX-87H Ion-exchange column (Bio-Rad Laboratories, Richmond, USA) was used, and its temperature was adjusted to 60 °C. The column was eluted with 5 mM sulfuric acid solution at 0.6 mL min^−1.

The ethanol yield was then determined according to Eq. (3), as follows [41]: FORMULA: Ethanol yield ( % ) = Ethanol concentration ( g l ) Glucan concentration ( g l ) × 1.111 × 0.51 × 100 where 1.111 is the conversion factor of glucan to glucose and 0.51 is the theoretical yield of ethanol from glucose.


2.5 Xanthan gum production

Xanthomonas campestris was obtained from the Persian type culture collection (PTCC 1473, Tehran, Iran). The cultivation and inoculum preparation were based on the method presented by Niknezhad et al. [43].

X. campestris was cultivated on the hydrolysate obtained from the enzymatic hydrolysis. The hydrolysate was supplemented with the following components (g L^−1): 2, NH_4NO_3; 2, KH_2PO_4; 2, C_6H_8O_7; 0.2, MgSO_4; 0.02, CaCl_2; 0.006, ZnCl_2; 0.006, H_3BO_3; and 0.0024, FeCl_3. Also, pH was set to 7. The fermentations were conducted in 500 mL shake flasks containing 50 mL of the medium at 30 °C for 72 h.

The produced xanthan gum was measured based on the method described elsewhere [44]. Briefly, all samples taken at the end of fermentation were centrifuged (Universal 320R, Hettich, Germany) at 8000 rpm for 20 min. The supernatant was then diluted with the triple volume of 96% ethanol to precipitate the xanthan gum. Afterward, the suspension was centrifuged at 4000 rpm for 15 min. The collected xanthan gum was then dried overnight at 60 °C.

Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy (FTIR) technique was also used to compare the produced xanthan gum structure with the commercial one. Commercial xanthan gum was the gum from X. campestris (Shandong Fufeng Biotechnology Co., Ltd, China).


2.6 Biogas production

Anaerobic digestion of the supernatant (the liquid fraction remaining after the organosolv pretreatment of PCR) and residual solids (solids remaining after enzymatic hydrolysis) was conducted in 118 mL dark glass bottles based on the method presented by Hansen et al. [45]. This method is among the most widely used ones for biomethane potential analysis; it has been previously used successfully for the measurement of the biomethane potential of sugarcane bagasse [46], orange waste [47], municipal solid wastes [38], safflower straw [22], and sunflower stalks [48]. Inoculum was obtained from the anaerobic digesters (7000 m^3) of the Isfahan Wastewater Treatment plant (Isfahan, Iran) that operated at 37 °C. Each bottle was filled with inoculum (20 mL) and the substrate (0.25 g based on the volatile solids, VS). In the case of residual solids, 5 mL of distilled water was also charged to achieve the mass ratio of 1:2 for the substrate to inoculum. According to the method used, no additional nutrient was required [45]. A blank sample consisting of only distilled water and inoculum was also performed to determine the biogas produced by the inoculum. Pure nitrogen was purged to each sealed bottle for 3 min to establish anaerobic conditions. The bottles were then incubated at 37 °C for 45 days. Samples were taken every 3–5 days until 45 days for biogas analysis.

A gas chromatography system (GC-2552, Teif Gostar Faraz Co., Iran) with a thermal conductivity detector was applied to analyze the CH_4 and CO_2 content of the produced biogas. The system was equipped with a 3 mm internal diameter × 3 m length packed column (stainless steel, Porapak Q, Chrompack, Germany). The accumulated biomethane produced during anaerobic digestions was presented in terms of mL methane per g VS of the substrate.

Experiments and measurements were conducted three times, and the averages (mean ± standard deviation) were reported.


2.7 Economic potential and gasoline equivalent calculation

The economic potential was calculated and discussed as a preliminary evaluation for the proposed biorefineries. The data generated in this study could serve as a basis for further detailed techno-economic studies.

To compare different pretreatments, the economic potential (EP) of the biofuels and xanthan gum for each case was calculated based on each ton of PCR [49]. The unit price of bioethanol and biogas was taken from the literature [37] and estimated as 0.85 $ L^−1 and 1 $ m^−3 for ethanol and biogas, respectively. In the case of xanthan gum, the lowest estimated price of 4 $ kg^−1 was applied, according to Mohsin et al. [50]. Also, the gasoline equivalent of the produced biofuels from all experiments was calculated based on each ton of PCR. The lower estimated heating values of 32 MJ L^−1, 22.1 MJ L^−1 and 36.1 MJ m^−3 were considered for gasoline, ethanol and methane, respectively [23].


2.8 Statistical analysis

Comparisons of means were made by conducting T-tests and ANOVA (analysis of variance). The analyses were conducted in Excel 2016. The level of significance was considered as 0.05 in all tests.


3 Results and discussion


3.1 Effects of organosolv pretreatment on the potato crop residue structure

The composition of the pretreated and untreated PCR and the solid recoveries can be seen in Table 1 .

The results showed that the glucan contents of the pretreated PCR (54.5–63.4%) were higher than those of the untreated one (48.2%). The highest glucan content (63.4%) was obtained from the pretreatment executed at 180 °C with 50% ethanol and in the presence of 1% catalyst. Moreover, the pretreatments with 50% ethanol and 1% H_2SO_4 at 140 and 120 °C resulted in the lower glucan contents of 58.4% and 56.5%, respectively. The significant difference between these glucan contents was certified by a two-sample t-test (p-value< 0.05). This implied that the glucan content could be improved by increasing the temperature of the pretreatment. Similar results were reported when rice straw was subjected to ethanol organosolv pretreatment at 150–180 °C for 60 min, where the glucan content of rice straw was improved from 54.8 to 62.3% [35].

The results, as shown in Table 1, revealed that temperature was a more influential factor than ethanol concentration and the presence of the catalyst. For instance, in the pretreatment with 50% ethanol without the catalyst, the rise of the temperature from 120 to 180 °C increased the glucan content from 55% to 62.8% (Table 1). However, the pretreatment at 180 °C with 75% ethanol in the absence of H_2SO_4 resulted in 61.6% glucan, while the same pretreatment in the presence of H_2SO_4 led to 62.9 ± 0.9% glucan, thus indicating no significant increase, with p-value >0.05. Moreover, the increase in the ethanol concentration from 50 to 75% was not able to improve the glucan content significantly. For instance, the glucan content detected for the pretreatment at 140 °C with 50% and 75% ethanol was very similar (58% and 57.8% respectively, with no significant increase, p-value <0.05).

The total lignin and xylan contents revealed that the pretreatments could considerably remove lignin and xylan from the PCR, as shown in Table 1. The highest lignin and xylan removal was observed after the pretreatment conducted at 180 °C with 50% ethanol concentration in the presence of 1% acid. Moreover, the highest glucan content was also attained in this pretreatment. Further, the results showed that the lignin and xylan contents were reduced by 16.7–27.6% and 9.3–70.32%, respectively (Table 1). Additionally, the xylan and lignin contents, as presented in Table 1, reflected that the higher temperature resulted in more reduction in the lignin and xylan contents. For instance, a significant decrease in the total lignin (23–18.4%) and xylan (15.3–5.7%) contents was observed when the temperature of the pretreatments with 50% ethanol was increased from 120 to 180 °C. The positive effect of temperature on lignin and xylan removal was also observed when wheat straw was pretreated with ethanol in the presence of H_2SO_4 as the catalyst [51].

The results of this study, thus, showed that the ethanol concentration and the presence of the catalyst had no significant influence on xylan and lignin removal. For instance, there were no significant differences (t-test, p-value >0.05) in total lignin and xylan contents between pretreatments at 180 °C with 75% ethanol, with or without the addition of the catalyst (Table 1).

The solid recoveries presented in Table 1 were in the range of 45.9–71.6%. It could be, therefore, deduced that the application of high temperature (180 °C) and the addition of the catalyst decreased solid recovery, whereas increasing the ethanol concentration enhanced the percentage of solid recovery. For example, the solid recovery obtained from the pretreatment with 75% ethanol at 180 °C (51.2%) was higher than that of the pretreatment at 120 °C (71.6%) (p-value <0.05).

To infer the morphological features as well as surface characterization of the untreated and treated samples, the analysis of SEM was performed (Fig. 2 ). The captured SEM images from the untreated (Fig. 2a) and treated sample (Fig. 2b) that resulted in the highest xanthan gum and ethanol yields are presented in Fig. 2. As can be seen, pretreatment clearly changed and modified the structure of PCR. The highly compact structure of the untreated PCR (Fig. 2a) became more roughen, sparse and porous (Fig. 2b) after the organosolv pretreatment process. This led to an increase in the accessible surfaces followed by cellulose availability for enzymatic and microbial attacks.


3.2 Enzymatic hydrolysis

The residual solids remaining after organosolv pretreatment were subjected to enzymatic hydrolysis. The obtained glucose yields are presented in Fig. 3 a. This figure shows that the glucose yield of 39.8–74.6% could be achieved when PCR was pretreated with ethanol. These yields were more than those attained from the untreated PCR (34.8%). The higher glucose yields obtained from the pretreated PCR could be related to the reduction in the lignin and xylan contents (Table 1), as observed in the previous studies [40]. Fig. 3a shows that the increase in the pretreatment temperature from 120 to 180 °C (in the treatment with 50% ethanol and in the absence of the catalyst) increased the glucose yield from 39.8% to 67.7% (two-sample t-test, p-value<0.05). However, the catalyst addition in the pretreatments had no significant influence on the glucose yield. For instance, the glucose yields obtained from the sample pretreated at 120 °C with 75% ethanol with and without catalyst were 39.9% and 41.0%, respectively (two-sample t-test, p-value<0.05).

It should be, however, noted that the increase in ethanol concentration from 50 to 75% had no significant effect on the glucose yield. For example, the glucose yield obtained from the sample pretreated with 50% ethanol at 120 °C was similar to that obtained from the pretreated one with 75% ethanol at 120 °C.

The highest glucose yield of 74.6% was attained when PCR was pretreated with the 50% ethanol solution at 180 °C in the presence of the catalyst. Nevertheless, the high glucose yield of 90% has been reported earlier when Populus was pretreated with 65% ethanol at 180 °C in the presence of 1% sulfuric acid [52]. Vergara et al. [53] also used dilute acid pretreatment at 130, 150, 160 and 170 °C for 45, 60 and 90 min with 1% H_2SO_4, as well as ethanol-water treatment at the same condition, with or without adding 1% of H 2 SO 4 as the catalyst. They reported a glucose yield of 80% from the pretreated wheat straw under both pretreatment methods. Comparing the glucose yields obtained in this study with the previous research indicated that PCR had a more recalcitrance structure than wheat straws and populous wood; however, more structural characterization is necessary.


3.3 Ethanol production

Fig. 3b illustrates the ethanol yields resulting from the ethanolic fermentation of the hydrolysates. The ethanol yields obtained from the pretreated samples (29.6–56.1%) were higher than those of the untreated sample (27.5%, Fig. 3). The maximum ethanol yield was obtained after the pretreatment with 50% ethanol and 1% acid at 180 °C. It was noteworthy that this yield (56.1%) was in the same order-of-magnitude as the ethanol yield obtained from organosolv pretreatment (50% ethanol concentration) of wheat straw at 180 °C (67%) [54] and sweet sorghum at 140 °C (72.5%) [29]. Moreover, Fig. 3b clearly shows that temperature was an effective factor on ethanol yield, whereas ethanol concentration and the presence of catalyst had no significant influence on the ethanol yield. For example, the ethanol yield was increased from 29.6 to 52.4 when the temperature was raised from 120 to 180 °C in the pretreatment with 50% ethanol and in the absence of the catalyst (two-sample t-test, p-value<0.05).

The results, as shown in Fig. 3b, were consistent with the glucose yields presented in Fig. 3 and xylan and lignin contents reported in Table 1. In other words, the pretreatments resulted in lower lignin contents and provided more accessibility to celluloses, thus resulting in higher glucose yields and subsequently, higher ethanol yields.


3.4 Xanthan gum production and characterization

The PCR hydrolysates were used for the microbial production of xanthan gum using X. campestris. The yields are presented in Fig. 4 . The results showed that the xanthan gum yield was considerably improved when PCR was subjected to organosolv pretreatment. The yields obtained from the untreated PCR (6.2 g xanthan gum per 100 g substrate) was much lower than those got from the pretreated PCR (7.8–12.5 g xanthan gum per 100 g substrate). The yields achieved in this study from PCR hydrolysates were in the same order of magnitude as those got from alkali-pretreated rice straw, i.e., 10.1 g xanthan gum per 100 g substrate [30].

Fig. 4 confirms that temperature was an influential factor on xanthan gum yields, whereas ethanol concentration and the presence of the catalysts had no significant influence. For instance, the gum yield obtained from the PCR pretreated at 180 °C with 50% ethanol in the absence of the catalyst (12.0 g xanthan gum per 100 g substrate from the pretreatment) was much higher than that got from the sample pretreated at 120 °C by using 50% ethanol concentration in the absence of the catalyst (7.8 g xanthan gum per 100 g substrate). This was also confirmed by the two-sample t-test (p-value<0.05). Moreover, the yields obtained from the samples pretreated with 50 and 75% ethanol in the absence of the catalyst at 120 °C (7.8 and 7.7 g xanthan gum per 100 g substrate, respectively) indicated no significant influence of the ethanol concentration on xanthan gum yields (two-sample t-test, p-value<0.05).

The FTIR spectra of the produced xanthan gum with the highest yield and the commercial xanthan gum are shown in Fig. 5 . These spectra were used for the qualitative comparison of the produced and commercial xanthan gum. It has been previously reported that the peak values related to the axial deformation of –OH, CO of enols, and C–O are in the range of (3200–3500), (1610–1640) and (1065–1085) cm^−1, respectively. Additionally, the peak related to the deflection angle of C–H has been found at 1420-1430 cm^−1 [44,50]. Fig. 5, interestingly, reveals similar peak values at the above-mentioned wave numbers. Moreover, the pack values of the commercial and produced xanthan gum related to hydroxyl, carbonyl, carboxyland, and acetyl groups were found at the wavenumbers of (3432,3433), (1610,1612), (1427,1434) and (1076,1079), respectively. Thus, it can be concluded that the functional groups of both xanthan gums were similar.


3.5 Anaerobic digestion

The accumulated methane yields obtained by the anaerobic digestion of the residual solids and the supernatant are illustrated in Fig. 6 . This figure shows that the anaerobic digestion of the residual solids that remained after the hydrolysis of the untreated PCR resulted in 164 mL methane g^−1 VS (Fig. 6a), which was the highest methane yield obtained from residual solids. However, the anaerobic digestion of the residual solids obtained from the pretreated PCR at 120 °C with 50% ethanol and the pretreated one at 180 °C with 50% ethanol resulted in 143 mL methane g^−1 VS and 122 mL methane g^−1 VS, respectively (Fig. 6b). This indicated that the increase in the temperature adversely affected the methane yield from residual solids. Nevertheless, the increase in the temperature had a positive influence on the production of methane from supernatants. Particularly, the increase of temperature from 140 to 180 °C (at the pretreatment with 50% ethanol) improved the methane yield from 148 to 196 mL methane g^−1 VS, respectively, which corresponded to 32% increase (Fig. 6d). This could be related to the fact that at high temperatures, e.g., 180 °C, a significant portion of hemicelluloses is released to the liquid fraction, thus resulting in the improved methane yield. However, the increase of temperature from 120 to 140 °C (at the pretreatment with 50% ethanol) resulted in only 11% improvement in the methane yield (133 mL methane g^−1 VS and 148 mL methane g^−1 VS, respectively). The highest methane yield measured in this study was 196 mL methane g^−1 VS, which was obtained from the supernatant of the pretreatment at 180 °C with 50% ethanol in the absence of the catalyst. This was in accordance with the yield reported earlier [22].


3.6 Economic potential and gasoline equivalent

The two scenarios presented in Fig. 1 were investigated in terms of gasoline equivalent and economic potential. The calculation was based on one ton of PCR as the feed. In scenario 1 (Fig. 1a), 70.3 kg xanthan gum and 59.1 m^3 methane (66.7 L equivalent gasoline) could be produced from each ton of PCR after the pretreatment at 140 °C with 50% ethanol and 1% acid. This corresponded to $330.7 total revenue and the economic potential of $280.7. In scenario 2 (Fig. 1b), 112.7 L ethanol (74.4 L equivalent gasoline) and 65 m^3 methane (73.3 L equivalent gasoline) were obtained after pretreatment at 180 °C with 75% ethanol without the catalyst. These products accounted for $157.4 revenue. Hence, the economic potential was $107.4. Therefore, the higher economic potential of scenario 1 indicated that the production of xanthan gum as a high-value product could have a higher industrial potential for the valorization of PCR.


4 Conclusions

Potato crop residues are produced in huge amounts every year in the world and most of them are left unused. Hence, this study investigated a sustainable process for the utilization of these residues for the production of xanthan gum and biofuels via integrated biorefining platforms. The obtained results indicated that xanthan gum and ethanol production yields from the untreated PCR were low. Nevertheless, organosolv pretreatments showed a promising positive influence on both xanthan gum and ethanol yields from PCR. The organosolv pretreatment at 180 °C with 50% ethanol in the presence of 1% H_2SO_4 resulted in the highest glucose (74.6%), ethanol (56.1%) and xanthan gum (12.5 g per 100 g substrate) yields. This also showed that the pretreatment increased sugar production yields, while no inhibitors were released or generated. Moreover, the residual solids and liquor were efficiently converted to biogas through anaerobic digestion. Overall, this study proposed a biorefinery approach for the valorization of PCR, thus serving a basis for the future techno-economic analysis, life cycle assessment, and scale-up.
