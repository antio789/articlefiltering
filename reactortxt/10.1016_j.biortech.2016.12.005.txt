TITLE: Exploitation of rapid acidification phenomena of food waste in reducing the hydraulic retention time (HRT) of high rate anaerobic digester without conceding on biogas yield


1 Introduction

The municipal solid waste (MSW) generation is increasing exponentially in developed and developing countries due to the immense economic development along with rapid population growth (Azadeh and Jalal, 2011; Rajesh et al., 2014). Biodegradable organic matter found in MSW that is termed as organic fraction of MSW (OFMSW) is mainly food waste (FW) which is collected from households, large kitchens and restaurants (Sergio and Aleiandra, 2015; Sameena et al., 2015; Surendra et al., 2014, 2016). Unscientific disposal of OFMSW leads to public health hazards due to the contamination of surface and groundwater, promoting the breeding of flies, mosquitoes, rats and other disease bearing vectors, emitting unpleasant odor (Das et al., 1998). FW (cooked food waste (CFW) and uncooked food waste (UFW)) generated from households, restaurants, institutional and industrial canteens etc., has a huge potential to generate bioenergy (Mata et al., 2000; Velmurugan and Ramanujam, 2011; Hyun et al., 2011; Sergio and Aleiandra, 2015; Sameena et al., 2015). Hence FW could be purposefully utilized for the generation of bioenergy through decentralized anaerobic digestion (AD) approach instead of disposing it off along with MSW. Therefore, attempts should be made to treat OFMSW at source in a remunerative manner so that the burden on local municipalities could be reduced drastically (Shin et al., 1999; Sameena et al., 2015). Extensive work was carried out on biomethanation of FW and it revealed that methane yield of 0.17–0.34 m^3/kg volatile solids (VS) could be achieved at a hydraulic residence time (HRT) of 30 days and volatile solids loading rate (VSLR) of 3.26 kg VS/m^3/day (Scott and Ma, 2004). However, most of the reports on AD of FW (Kirtane et al., 2009) for the treatment of smaller quantity in the range of 100–250 kg/day are based on either conventional digester designs typically used for cattle manure or plug flow digester (Demirer and Chen, 2004). These designs could not be scaled up to treat higher quantities of FW about 1000 kg/day and above (Rajesh and Sounak, 2013). It was reported that during biomethanation of FW in a single-phase digester, there was drop in pH due to (Curry and Pillay, 2011) rapid acidification phenomena and hence bi-phasic systems were proposed (kirtane et al., 2009). However, in the recent work carried out by Sameena et al. (2015), rapid acidification phenomena associated with the AD of FW (CFW and UFW separately) was effectively exploited for the reduction of HRT of the digester from 30 days (Scott and Ma, 2004) to 10 days in a laboratory scale single phase anaerobic digester to get the biogas yield of 0.47 m^3/(kg VS reduction) and 0.38 m^3/(kg VS reduction) from CFW and UFW respectively. In continuation to this work, a full scale biogas plant was designed and installed based on an innovative high rate digester called “Anaerobic Gas lift Reactor” (AGR) (Rao et al., 2012, 2015) for the combined treatment of 1000 kg of FW (CFW and UFW) and 2000 L of rice gruel (RG) per day. In the present communication, the detailed performance of the plant in terms of HRT (days), VSLR (kg VS/m^3/day), organic loading rate (OLR) (kg COD/m^3/day), buffering capacity, effect of C/N ratio, biogas yield (m^3/day), VS reduction (%),COD reduction (%), methane yield (m^3/(kg VS reduced) & m^3/(kg COD reduced)), recycle management, bio manure (kg/day) productivity etc., is presented.


2 Materials and methods


2.1 Plant installation

The Akshaya Patra Foundation TAPF (2016) (https://www.akshayapatra.org/kitchen-addresses, last accessed on 10 Sep 2016) is a non-governmental organization (NGO) operates under the aegis of ISKON, serves mid-day meals to school children in India. There are several large-scale kitchens setup by TAPF across India and one of their kitchens is at JINDAL Township, Torangallu, Bellary District, Karnataka, India. M/s Ahuja Engineering Services Private Limited AESPL (2016) (https://www.linkedin.com/company/ahuja-engineering-services-pvt-ltd, last accessed on 10 Sep 2016), Secunderabad, has installed and commissioned a biogas plant in March 2015 for the treatment of FW and RG based on AGR technology at the aforesaid place.


2.2 Feedstock for the plant

Vegetarian food is prepared for about 1,00,000 children at the kitchen of TAPF. UFW is a combination of fruit and vegetable waste, whereas CFW is edible cooked food which is uneaten and spoiled. FW (400 kg of CFW and 600 kg of UFW) was collected from the kitchen of TAPF and used as feedstock for the plant. In addition to this, RG (water separated from the rice after cooking), a liquid wastewater also generated from the same kitchen (2000 L/day) was also mixed with FW for feeding into the reactor.


2.3 Description of the plant

The plant consists of a 2.7 m^3 feed preparation tank (FPT), a 42 m^3 anaerobic gas lift reactor (AGR) (liquid volume (29.4 m^3) and gas volume (12.6 m^3)), 32 m^3 biogas balloon (BB), 2 m^3 medium pressure (8 kg/cm^2) intermediate biogas storage tank (MPBST), a 2.7 m^3 recycle collection tank (RCT), a gas compressor and a moisture trap (MT). AGR, MPBST and MT were fabricated with mild steel (MS), FPT and RCT were made of bricks and cement. Pumps, compressor and BB (made of poly propylene) were procured from a local vendor. Every day CFW was directly fed to the FPT where as UFW was grounded before loading to FPT. Subsequently, the feed slurry after mixing with milk of lime (MOL) and recycle (outlet from the AGR) fed to the AGR by maintaining the pH in the range of 6.8–7.2. Biogas generated in the AGR was passed through MT and stored in BB. Later on it was compressed, stored in MPBST and pipelined to cooking stoves. The remaining digestate (outlet from AGR) after recycle was sun dried and used as bio manure.


2.4 Description of high rate digester - AGR

AGR is a vertical cylindrical tank with an aspect ratio of 2:1 with two compartments, which were hydraulically connected. The influent was pumped into the bottom compartment of the reactor using an efficient distribution system, and was mixed with anaerobic biomass. In the lower part of the reactor most of the organic components were converted into methane (CH_4) and carbon dioxide (CO_2). The biogas generated at the bottom compartment (1st stage) creates sufficient pressure that forces the liquid upwards through the riser into the upper compartment (2nd stage). A three-phase separator comprising baffles, an inverted cone and a downer pipe on top of the reactor separates biogas, active biomass and liquid respectively. Biogas leaves the reactor through the three-phase separator, the slurry returns through the downer to the bottom of the system. Active biomass falls back to the reactor and the treated liquid leaves the reactor through the outlet. This action takes place continuously at regular intervals based on the set pressure. Biogas collected at the top of the reactor is partially recycled and injected at the bottom of the reactor to boost the pressure inside the lower compartment that would increase the number of mixing cycles. Due to this phenomenon, total slurry in the digester was mixed thoroughly. This mixing enhances the volatile solids degradation rate (VSDR), heat transfer between particles occur thereby increases the biogas production and reduces the formation of scum in the digester. The number of mixing cycles per day depends upon the biogas production potential of FW, set pressure value, temperature, VSLR and VSDR. Three-phase separator mechanism delinks the solids retention time (SRT) and HRT so that active biomass could be retained as much as possible to enhance the microbial activity, whereas the liquid could be pushed out to handle higher throughput. AGR was operated under ambient temperature conditions and a provision was made for measuring slurry temperature of the reactor outlet.


2.5 Preparation and loading of inoculum to AGR

AGR was initially seeded with 10 m^3 of active methanogenic sludge that was obtained from an anaerobic lagoon, which was being operated for the treatment of distillery wastewater (spent wash) at a distillery (Bellary, Karnataka, India). The Volatile Suspended Solids (VSS) of the seed sludge was 15.45 g/L and the methanogenic activity was 0.22 g CH_4-COD/g VSS/day (Gangagni Rao et al., 2013).


2.6 Analytical methods

FW was initially characterized for pH, total solids (TS), volatile solids (VS), moisture content (MC), Chemical oxygen demand (COD), Biochemical oxygen demand (BOD), carbon (C), Hydrogen (H), Nitrogen (N) and sulfur(S) as per the standard methods (APHA-AWWA-WPCF, 1998) on weekly basis as per the requirement. In order to determine the COD of FW (CFW or UFW), 500 ml of water was mixed with 500 g of FW and grounded to make slurry, the sample from the slurry was used for estimating COD. The quantity of C, H and N was estimated with CHNS analyzer (Vario Micro Cube Elementar, Germany). The volume of biogas produced was measured and the composition was estimated using an Orsat apparatus (APHA-AWWA-WPCF, 1998; Orsat, 1875). Analysis is carried out in triplicate and the variation is found to be within ±1.0%. All the chemicals used for analysis during the experiments were of analaR (AR) grade reagents.


2.7 Equivalence of COD and VS

It is known that COD is a measure of organic matter in the liquid waste whereas in case of solid waste VS represents the organic matter (Mogens and Yves, 2008). In the present case, both liquid waste (RG) and solid waste (FW) were fed to the AGR. In order to estimate the VSLR and OLR of AGR, equivalence factor of VS and COD for each component of FW (CFW and UFW) and RG were determined based on the characteristics (Table 1). It is evident from Table 1 that CFW was having 21% of VS and 72,000 mg/L of COD. Therefore, 105 g of VS in CFW (21% of 500 g) is equivalent to 72 g of COD. This essentially means that 1 g of VS in CFW is equivalent to 0.69 g of COD. Similarly, 1 g of VS in UFW and RG is equivalent to 0.88 g and 1.0 g of COD respectively. The VSLR and OLR were calculated based on the above criteria when the AGR was fed with combined wastes (CFW, UFW and RG).


2.8 Start-up and operational procedure of the plant

Initially, 100 kg of UFW was grounded to reduce the particle size in the range of 200 μm, mixed with 100 kg of CFW and soaked overnight in FPT. The following day, soaked FW (UFW and CFW) was mixed with required amount of water to make the slurry TS in the range of 10–12%. The slurry pH (in the range of 5.2–5.8) was adjusted in the range of 7.0–7.2 with milk of lime (MOL) and the same was loaded into the reactor. This process was repeated everyday till AGR was filled up to the full volume of 29.4 m^3. Subsequent to this, AGR was operated continuously and fed with same amount of FW (100 kg of CFW and 100 kg of UFW) by maintaining the VSLR of 0.7 kg VS/m^3/day and an OLR of 0.6 kg COD/m^3/day (calculated as per Section 2.7). After stabilizing the reactor (in terms of biogas yield and VS reduction) at these VSLR and OLR, feed rate was increased in a stepwise manner from 200 kg/day (100 kg of CFW + 100 kg of UFW) (this corresponds to VSLR and OLR of 0.7 kg VS/m^3/day and 0.6 kg COD/m^3/day respectively) to 600 kg/day (300 kg of CFW and 300 kg of UFW) (this corresponds to VSLR and OLR of 2.2 kg VS/m^3/day and 1.7 kg COD/m^3/day respectively) within a span of 30 days. At each VSLR (OLR), AGR was operated until a steady state condition was reached; steady state condition was characterized by a stable biogas production (±5%) and VS reduction (±8%). However, when the VSLR and OLR were increased beyond 2.2 kg VS/m^3/day and 1.7 kg COD/m^3/day respectively (corresponds to 600 kg/day of FW), the pH of the digester outlet dropped and generation of biogas fell down well below the normal range. At this juncture, feeding of FW to AGR was stopped for a week and digester was put on recycle mode (outlet of the digester was recycled) along with the addition of MOL as per the requirement to make the pH neutral. The pH inside the digester was improved during this phase and gradually FW was loaded again to the AGR in the same manner as it was done earlier. However, during this phase of operation, a part of the outlet from AGR was mixed with feed and recycled (feed to recycle ratio 1:1) to the digester. Additionally, the pH of the slurry was adjusted to neutral range with the aid of MOL. In this pattern loading to the AGR was increased gradually to the level 1000 kg/day (400 kg CFW and 600 kg UFW) which corresponds to a VSLR of 3.8 kg VS/m^3/day and OLR of 2.9 kg COD/m^3/day. Successively, water used for preparing the FW slurry was replaced with RG in a phased manner from 500 L to 2000 L per day generated at that kitchen. Finally, 1000 kg of FW along with 2000 L of RG per day was fed to the reactor which corresponds to a VSLR and OLR of 4.9 kg VS/m^3/day and 4.0 kg COD/m^3/day at full load of operation. The biogas produced was measured using a wet gas flow meter, passed through moisture trap and stored in BB. The biogas stored in BB was used for thermal applications (for cooking meals in the kitchen) after it was compressed in MPBST. Methane content in the biogas was measured with Orsat apparatus.


3 Results and discussion


3.1 Feedstock characteristics

In general, the characteristics of FW depends on eating habits of people living in that area and same is the case with food waste generated in the kitchen at TAPF, Bellary. Therefore, FW (UFW, CFW) and RG used in the present study were characterized for various parameters and the same was tabulated and is shown in Table 1 . It could be noted from Table 1 that the COD of CFW, UFW and RG were 72,000 mg/L, 44,000 mg/L and 16,000 mg/L respectively. It could also be deduced from Table 1 that the components of FW (CFW, UFW) and RG are highly biodegradable since VS/TS ratio and BOD/COD ratio for CFW, UFW and RG were 0.9, 0.83, 0.94 and 0.84, 0.97, 1.0 respectively. It could be understood from the characteristics that the aforesaid substrates are extremely suitable for bioenergy generation through AD approach. Nitrogen (N), an essential nutrient required for AD was not available either in CFW or RG, where as it was approximately 0.75% in UFW. It is worth mentioning here that C/N ratio of combined FW (CFW and UFW) along with RG (at 100% waste utilization) was in the range of 25–30 which is well within the optimum ratio (20–30) required for biomethanation (Musa et al., 2014). It could also be comprehended from the characteristics (Table 1) that combined waste (CFW, UFW and RG) is highly suitable for the generation of biogas and bio manure.


3.2 Feedstock composition, phenomena of rapid acidification and maintenance of pH in FPT

It is known from the characteristics (Table 1) that the pH of CFW, UFW and RG was 5.21, 6.5 and 7.0 respectively. In order to feed the FW slurry to AGR, UFW was grounded, mixed with CFW and water and then soaked in FPT overnight (Section 2.6). The pH of the FW slurry was found to be in the range of 5.2–5.5 after soaking for 12 h, despite the fact that pH of UFW (pH was 6.5) and water (pH was 7.4) was close to neutral. It was understood from this phenomenon that, feed slurry immediately after soaking (12 h) was getting acidified. Therefore, a graph is drawn for the ratios of VFA/COD and VFA/Alkalinity before and after acidification along with pH for the entire period of operation and is shown in Fig. 1 . It could be understood from Fig. 1, that VFA/COD before acidification was in the range of 0.03 where as it was increased to 0.4 after acidification. This essentially means that most of the COD was getting converted to VFA’s within a period of 12 h due to rapid acidification as CFW and RG predominantly contained carbohydrates (Manni et al., 2012; McCance and Widdowson’s, 2015). The rapid acidification phenomena that was observed in our case was worthy from the perspective of giving readily available food (in terms of VFA) to the methanogens in the digester and decreasing the time of digestion (HRT) of biodegradable organic matter. However, this phenomenon could cause instability in the digester (pH was in the range of 5.0–5.5) unless buffering capacity of the digester is enhanced externally. Therefore, during this period, MOL was added in the FPT to maintain the pH of the feed slurry around 7.0 and pH of the reactor outlet was found to be in the range of 7.1–7.2. As stated previously (Section 2.6), during this period, plant was operated as once through cycle (without recycling the outlet slurry of AGR) and FW load could be not be increased beyond 600 kg/day in spite of the addition of approximately 3.6 kg of MOL per day for neutralization of the slurry.


3.3 Resolving the issue of instability due to souring while stabilizing the AGR at full load

The pattern of OLR, VSLR, pH and biogas generation during the operational period (68 weeks) is graphically shown in Fig. 2 . It could be learnt from Fig. 2 that when the VSLR was increased to 2.2 kg VS/m^3/day (600 kg FW/day), the pH of the reactor outlet dropped to 6.5 (Fig. 2) even though the pH of the inlet feed slurry was maintained at around 7.0 by the addition of MOL in FPT. Besides this biogas generation also declined to 30 m^3/day (Fig. 2) indicating instability in the digester. At this juncture, to ascertain the reason for low outlet pH of the digester, samples were drawn from different points of the plant (FPT, all the ports of the digester and outlet) and analyzed for pH, VFA, alkalinity, COD etc., and the results are shown in Table 2 . In general, in a stable digester, pH and COD reduction would be showing rising trend where as VFA/Alkalinity ratio should be decreasing from bottom of the digester to top. Nevertheless, in the present case, pH was 7.0 in FPT where as it was 6.5 in the outlet showing decreasing trend in the ports of the digester (Table 2). Similarly, VFA was observed to be higher than alkalinity in all the ports and outlet with very marginal increase in reduction of COD across the digester. The composition of the biogas was also found to be rich in CO_2 (65% CO_2 and 35% CH_4) at this crisis and this was ascertained with the inconsistency in color in the flame of the burner (when the methane content is above 60%, the flame was bluish where as when the methane content fell below 45%, the flame was reddish). It was learnt from these occurrences that the digester was souring while loading beyond the VSLR of 2.2 kg VS/m^3/day. The feed to the digester was stopped and digester was put on back to back recycle (reactor outlet was collected in FPT and fed back to the reactor). It was understood during this course of operation that mere addition of MOL and maintenance of pH of the inlet feed slurry in the range of 7.0 was not enough to maintain the buffering capacity of the digester for stable operation beyond the VSLR of 2.2 kg VS/m^3/day. Hence, when the digester was re-started with FW of 200 kg/day, AGR outlet from RCT was recycled (partially) to FPT and mixed with feed in the ratio of 1:1 apart from adding MOL in the FPT. In this pattern, the digester could be operated at full load of 1000 kg FW per day (Fig. 3 ). This recycle mechanism apart from stabilizing the outlet pH of the digester in the range of 7.2–7.4, aided the plant to be operated stably and continuously at this load yielding biogas of 120 m^3/day at a VSLR and OLR of 3.8 kg VS/m^3/day and 2.9 kg COD/m^3/day respectively (calculated as per Section 2.7). The pH of the feed slurry after recycling was in the range of 7.2–7.4, ratio’s of VFA/Alkalinity and VFA/COD were in the range of 7–9 and 0.4–0.6 respectively (Fig. 1). The recycling of outlet slurry back to AGR (feed to recycle in the 1:1 ratio) resulted in maintaining the pH in the range of 6.8–7.1 in the FPT and MOL consumption remained at 3.6 kg per day even though feeding of FW was increased from 600 to 1000 kg/day. In the successive phase, when water was replaced with RG (pH-7.0) in a phased manner from 500 L–2000 L per day the consumption of MOL was further reduced to 3 kg/day and pH of the AGR outlet was maintained in the range of 7.2–7.4.


3.4 Effect of co-digestion on biogas yield

It is known that co-digestion is preferred when different substrates having complementary characteristics (mainly in terms of C and N) are available (Dan and Yebo, 2013; McCance and Widdowson’s, 2015) for AD. In the present case, CFW, UFW and RG were available at site in such a proportion (CFW- 400 kg, UFW- 600 kg and RG-2000 L) that it was very advantageous to blend them for feeding into the AGR to obtain the desired performance of the digester in terms of methane yield at low HRT and high OLR. It could be seen from Fig. 3 that 1000 kg of FW (400 kg CFW and 600 kg UFW) per day was loaded into the reactor at a VSLR and OLR of 3.8 kg VS/m^3/day and 2.9 kg COD/m^3/day respectively and this resulted in biogas yield of 100 to120 m^3/day. Later on when 2000 L of RG containing notable COD (Table 1) was added to 1000 kg of FW, an additional production of biogas (15–20 m^3/day) was observed and is shown in Fig. 3. Generally, a C/N ratio ranging between 20 and 30 was considered to be optimum for AD (Musa et al., 2014). The C/N ratio of UFW was less than the optimum value (Table 1). To enhance the C/N ratio of the feed, UFW was blended with CFW, RG and the reactor outlet. After blending CFW with UFW in required proportions (2:3) and RG, the C/N ratio of the feed considerably improved and was found to be ranging between 25 and 30. The blending proportion of CFW and UFW was maintained 2:3 because, the pH of CFW was comparatively less than UFW (Table 1) and CFW predominantly contained carbohydrates (Chuanyang et al., 2016; Cunsheng et al., 2013). This is one of the reasons for obtaining higher methane yields. Hence, co-digestion of FW along with RG (400 kg CFW + 600 kg UFW + 2000 L RG) enhanced the biogas yield due to the reasons of complementary characteristics of feedstock to obtain optimum C/N ratio.


3.5 Effect of rapid acidification on HRT

It was reported that a major limitation of AD of FW (Curry and Pillay, 2011) is the rapid acidification phenomena which could lead to accumulation of VFA’s that reduce the methanogenic activity of the reactor (Sameena et al., 2015). Besides this, it was also understood in the present work that during the feed pre-processing in FPT, most of the COD was getting converted into organic acids within a period of 12 h (Fig. 1). Hence, keeping this in view, feed slurry was neutralized with MOL so that under these conditions the population of methanogens could build up and enhance the activity that would result in higher degradation of organic acids to methane, CO_2 and stabilized organic manure. However, contrary to the contemplation, digester showed negative trend in terms of pH, biogas yield and VS/COD degradation when it was loaded beyond 600 kg of FW per day. The methanogenic activity of the reactor was estimated when digester was under souring conditions and found that it dropped from 0.22 g CH_4-COD/g VSS/day to 0.1 g CH_4-COD/g VSS/day. This indicated that during this phase, acidification could have occurred rapidly inside the digester since the growth rate of acidogens is faster than methanogens (Fayyaz et al., 2014) within a span 5 to 7 days. The digester was brought back to normal condition by re-circulating the reactor outlet back to AGR for a week and then it was fed with substrate after the addition of MOL at a VSLR of 0.7 kg VS/m^3/day, OLR of 0.6 kg COD/m^3/day at an HRT of 49 days (Figs. 2 and 4 ). The only change that was made during this phase was that along with the feed, digester outlet was re-circulated back at 1:1 (feed to recycle) ratio. Gradually, the methanogenic activity inside the reactor was enhanced (0.20 g CH_4-COD/g VSS/day) with an increase in biogas yield (Fig. 3). The reactor was stabilized at a VSLR of 4.9 kg VS/m^3/day, OLR of 4.0 kg COD/m^3/day at HRT of 10 days after 30 weeks (Figs. 2 and 4). The AGR could be operated consistently and continuously for another 37 weeks at the same loading rate and HRT of 10 days (Fig. 4). Readily available organic acids were consumed by methanogens for the production of biogas and in this phase of operation the digester could be fed with 100% of FW available at site (1000 kg/day). Apart from this, digester could also be operated continuously and stably at this load by obtaining the methane yield in the range of 0.56 m^3/kg VS reduced and 0.6 m^3/kg COD reduced at an HRT of 10 days (Fig. 4). It was realized that rapid acidification phenomenon of FW was occurring in the FPT (Section 3.4) due to the fact that, the VFA/COD before acidification was 0.03 whereas it increased to 0.4 after acidification (Fig. 1). This essentially means that, most of the COD is getting converted to VFA in FPT itself with in 12 h. In addition to this, it was understood that methanogenesis was taking place inside the digester that was ascertained by the methanogenic activity (0.20 g CH_4-COD/g VSS/day) of the microbes inside the digester and biogas generation of 130–140 m^3/day at an HRT of 10 days (Fig. 4). An HRT of 10 days was reported earlier for the production of biogas from FW (Sameena et al., 2015), and the HRT in present study was also estimated to be 10 days which is in congruence with the earlier studies. Nevertheless, the present study showed better results in comparison with the study carried out by Zhang et al. (2007) for FW, where it was reported that 435 ml of biogas per gram VS obtained at an HRT of 28 days. Hence, it could be concluded from the full scale operating experience that, though the substrate has a limitation of undergoing rapid acidification, the acidified slurry could be fed to the digester having methanogenic bacteria after adjusting the pH with MOL and recycle to get the maximum biogas yield of 140 m^3/day at a low HRT (10 days).


3.6 Performance of the plant in terms of biogas generation, methane yield and VS reduction (%) and COD reduction (%)

The plant is in continuous operation since August 2015 to till date after stabilizing the reactor and establishing a constant OLR (calculated as per Section 2.7) and VSLR of 4.0 kg COD/m^3/day and 4.9 kg VS/m^3/day respectively. The performance of AGR in terms of biogas yield (m^3/day), HRT (days), COD reduction (%), VS reduction (%) and methane yield (m^3 CH_4/(kg VS reduced) & m^3 CH_4/(kg COD reduced)) is graphically shown in Figs. 2 and 4 for 68 weeks (up to July 2016). It could be deduced from Figs. 2 and 4 is that, during 5th week the biogas yield, VS reduction (%) and COD reduction (%) dropped down from 72 m^3/day to 30 m^3/day, 70% to 55% and 80% to 70% respectively during acidification stage (formation of organic acids) of the digester. It could also be interpreted from Figs. 2 and 4 is that after stabilization, the average biogas yield (m^3/day), VS reduction (%), COD reduction (%) obtained were in the range 130–140 m^3/day and 70–76%, 82–88% respectively at an HRT of 10 days. The methane yields were found to be 0.56 m^3/kg VS reduced and 0.6 m^3/(kg COD reduced) during the stable period of operation (August 2015–July 2016) when the average temperature recorded was 31 °C. The performance summary of the biogas plant for a period of 68 weeks under stable operating conditions is presented in Table 3 .


3.7 Mass balance under stable conditions

It is essential to determine the mass balance of the system apart from optimizing the design parameters such as VSLR, OLR, HRT etc., to establish the outputs from the plant against input quantity of FW. Mass balance analysis (average values) was carried out for 68 weeks i.e. from August 2015 to July 2016. The inlet and outlet mass of the system is graphically illustrated and is shown in Fig. 5 . On an average, FW along with RG (400 kg CFW + 600 kg UFW + 2000 L RG) having 245 kg of TS, 205 kg of VS, 167 kg COD (calculated as per Section 2.7) and 40 kg of inorganic solids (IS) were fed to AGR. This feed actually yielded biogas of 140 m^3/day along with 110 kg of bio manure (40 kg IS + 50 kg VS + 20 kg COD) per day. Nevertheless, in the present work, a high rate biomethanation plant based on AGR yielded average methane of 0.56 m^3 CH_4/(kg VS reduced) and 0.6 m^3 CH_4/(kg COD reduced) per day from 155 kg of VS and 147 kg COD ‘at an average temperature of 31 °C. Fig. 5 elucidates the mass balance under stable conditions in terms of TS, VS, COD and IS and the output from the reactor in terms of biogas and bio manure.


3.8 Biogas storage and its equivalent calorific value

Biogas generated from AGR was passed through moisture trap (to remove moisture), stored in BB and was sent to MPBST. Biogas from MPBST pipelined to the same kitchen, used for cooking meals to replace LPG. The composition of biogas was analyzed and found to contain 60% CH_4 and 40% CO_2. The calorific value of biogas and LPG is 5000 kcal/m^3 and 10,800 kcal/kg respectively at NTP (Peter, 2009). It was known that at maximum load (100% of FW and RG utilization), biogas yield of 140 m^3/day was obtained containing 84 m^3 of CH_4 which is equivalent to 67.68 kg of LPG.


4 Conclusions

AGR was operated continuously after stabilization at VSLR of 4.9 kg VS/m^3/day (OLR of 4.0 kg COD/m^3/day) with FW and RG. Lower pH of the substrate was appropriately addressed by neutralizing the feed to the AGR with the addition of MOL and recycle. The occurrence of rapid acidification phenomena was effectively exploited for the reduction of HRT to 10 days. It was derived from the consistent performance of the plant that methane yield of 0.56 m^3 CH_4/(kg VS reduced) (0.6 m^3 CH_4/(kg COD reduced)) could be obtained at a VS reduction of 76% (COD reduction of 88%). Biogas generated at this plant was used for cooking to replace LPG. The techno-commercial aspects of the biogas plant are shown in Table 4 .
