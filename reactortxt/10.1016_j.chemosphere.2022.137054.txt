TITLE: An integrated leachate bed reactor – anaerobic membrane bioreactor system (LBR-AnMBR) for food waste stabilization and biogas recovery


1 Introduction

Sustainable management of food waste (FW) is a global challenge which impacts the environment and economy alike. Production of municipal solid waste (MSW) globally is 2.1 billion tons per year and is estimated to increase to 3.4 billion tons by 2050. FW comprises 30–60% of the MSW generated (World Bank, 2018; Hussain et al., 2021; Kandakatla et al., 2017). Canadians produce over 400 kg of FW annually on per capita basis, making them one of the highest generators of FW in the world. The associated losses to the Canadian economy are estimated to be over $31 billion a year (Government of Ontario, 2017). The current FW disposal, that is landfilling, composting pyrolysis, incineration and so on, can pose threats to human health & environment. Landfilling is widely used for FW disposal in Canada due to its simplicity and low cost, but it significantly contributes to the national methane emissions (National Zero Waste Council, 2018). Landfill leachate has contaminated subsurface environments with severe consequences to the human health and ecology (Iqbal et al., 2022; Government of Ontario, 2017; Thi et al., 2015). FW is wet biomass, and thus incineration and pyrolysis need high energy input and requires costly treatment of the stack gases to prevent air pollution (Abd Kadir et al., 2013; Liu et al., 2015).

Anaerobic digestion (AD) has grown as the alternative solution to FW disposal. There are a variety of AD technologies and among them two-stage AD has high and stable treatability (Srisowmeya et al., 2020). Two-stage AD processes consist of hydrolysis/acidogenesis step followed by methanogenesis, which effectively enrich fermenters and methanogens in each step (Srisowmeya et al., 2020), ensuring high treatability even at high organic loading rates (OLRs). Continuously stirred tank reactors (CSTRs) have been widely used as the hydrolytic and acidogenic reactor in two-stage ADs, but CSTRs need a high capital and operating cost for stabilization of high solid FW; water dilution to prepare <10% solid concentration of FW feedstock is essential for CSTR operation, which increases operating costs and wastewater generation (Browne et al., 2013; Xiong et al., 2019b). High-strength wastewater effluent from the traditional two-stage CSTR ADs can cause serious problems in FW management. High concentrations of organics and nutrients in the FW wastewater do not allow direct discharge to sewers or water bodies. Post-treatment processes to this wastewater will add significant investment and operation costs. Alternatively, municipalities have regularly trucked the wastewater and discharged it to landscape, which would be neither economical nor sustainable. Moreover, this current practice will cause secondary pollution in soil and groundwater and threaten human health due to microbial pathogens (Hubbard et al., 2022; Jones et al., 2021; City of Vancouver, 2012).

In recent years, dry fermentation has gained attention as a low-cost hydrolytic and acidogenic reactor. Leach-bed reactors (LBRs), one of dry fermentation processes, offer many advantages over CSTRs for treatment of solid organic waste including less energy consumption, and separation of FW solid from microbial enrichment. The leachate produced in LBRs have high concentrations of volatile fatty acids (VFAs) which can be easily recovered as biogas to establish a sustainable and cost-effective FW management system (Shewa et al., 2020; Xiong et al., 2019a). No water dilution is required for LBRs, which means minimum wastewater generation in FW treatment; this is of significance to post-treatment of wastewater. LBRs can be integrated with conventional CSTR AD for two-stage FW stabilization and methane recovery, but the effluent quality from these systems are too poor to protect water bodies, especially for microbial pathogens and particulate contaminants. Anaerobic membrane bioreactors (AnMBRs) can be used for the second-stage AD process to improve effluent water quality, since membrane separation can remove pathogens and particulates. Another benefit of AnMBRs is to shorten hydraulic retention time (HRT) with long solid retention time (SRT), improving biogas productivity with less footprint of AD (Galib et al., 2016; Lin et al., 2013). This feature of AnMBR (decoupling HRT and SRT) can provide another advantage, i.e., high COD removal and biogas yields at room conditions of 21–22 °C (psychrophilic conditions). AD have generally been operated at mesophilic (35–40 °C) and/or thermophilic temperatures (50–55 °C). Such operating conditions are suitable for locations with warmer climates but require external heating in colder conditions (e.g., Canada), which can significantly impact the net-energy gain as well as the reduction in GHG emissions. Therefore, characterization of AnMBR performance at room conditions (i.e., without external heating) is of interest.

There are several challenges of AnMBRs, and one of them is their expensive cost, as compared to existing ADs due to membranes; membrane installation and maintenance will add costs to conventional ADs (Lee and Liao, 2021). Short HRTs or extensive SRTs can lead to severe membrane fouling and reduce permeate flux and membrane lifetime. Maintenance cleaning of membranes can be used to keep desirable permeate flux. Frequent cleaning and use of high concentration of cleaning agents, however, could cause severe damage to membranes and inhibition of the activity of microorganisms because most of the cleaning agents are strong oxidants, acids, or bases (Judd, 2017; Lei et al., 2018). These AnMBR challenges related to membrane fouling become significant especially for high solid waste treatment including FW, which has limited AnMBR application to treatment of high solid organic waste (Lee and Liao, 2021). However, AnMBRs can be the ideal second-stage methanogenesis reactor when they are combined with LBRs because of solid-liquid separation in LBR design (Hussain et al., 2017; Shewa et al., 2020); The LBR feature of solid-liquid separation can minimize the drawback of AnMBRs related to membrane fouling. In addition, pathogen-free, but nutrients-rich AnMBR permeates can be used for liquid fertilizers or irrigation water for landscapes.

We report a first study of an integrated LBR-AnMBR system for FW treatment and biogas recovery. First, FW hydrolysis and acidogenesis was maximized in an LBR by optimizing the leachate recirculation rate (4.4–13.2 L/h). Second, the HRT (13–25 d), SRT (75–130 d) and permeate flux (0.45–6 LMH) of an AnMBR was optimized on FW leachate from the LBR. Third, microbial community of the two bioreactors was analyzed to understand the functions of the microorganisms in the individual reactors, and finally, the integrated two-stage system (LBR-AnMBR) was investigated and optimized FW stabilization and biogas recovery.


2 Materials and methods


2.1 Reactor design

An LBR and AnMBR were employed for this work (Fig. 1 ). The LBR was fabricated with acrylic materials with a total volume of 11 L (inner diameter: 14 cm, outer diameter: 15.25 cm, height: 72 cm). The LBR was composed of three parts: (1) a top cover and a sprinkler system to spray leachate evenly on loaded FW, (2) a FW container in the middle, and (3) a bottom part having the leachate. The FW container was prepared with polyvinylchloride (PVC) materials (inner diameter: 12.75 cm and height: 21.6 cm) and its effective volume was 1 L. A mesh with a pore size of 4 mm supported the FW container to hold the FW particles until they were hydrolyzed to fall into the leachate on the bottom.

The lab-scale AnMBR was fabricated with PVC (inner diameter: 10.3 cm, height: 69 cm), and the working volume was 5 L. The AnMBR was equipped with side ports for feeding, sludge circulation and pH/temperature monitoring sensors. Hollow-fibre ultrafiltration (UF) membrane modules (BlueOcean Memtech Pte Ltd., Singapore) with an average pore size was 0.04 μm was used in this study. The UF membranes were immersed in the AnMBR (Fig. 1). Two membranes modules having different surface areas (0.0174 m^2 for membrane 1 and 0.0024 m^2 for membrane 2) were tested for the AnMBR to evaluate low to high membrane fluxes (0.45–6 L/m^2-h) during the experiments.

The biogas production in LBR and AnMBR was measured using gas counters (MilliGas counter, Ritter Apparatus, Bochum, Germany). Neutral pH was kept in the two-bioreactor using a pH controller (Milwaukee, MC-122 pH meter) connected to a NaOH dosing pump.


2.2 Experimental setup

In this study both LBR and AnMBR were operated 22 ± 0.5 °C (room temperature). The pH of both the reactors were maintained at neutral conditions (pH of 7 ± 0.1 for LBR and 7.3 ± 0.2 for AnMBR). Fig. 1 shows individual bioreactors and the process flow diagram for the integrated system. At the start of the experiment, 1 kg of FW was loaded into the LBR along with pre-heated AD sludge. Deionized water was used was dilution to meet the desired inoculum to substrate ratio (ISR) of 10% (g VS/g VS). The following runs in the LBR were only operated with enriched sludge obtained by centrifuging LBR leachate at 9500 rpm for 20 min. To maintain ISR of 10% and initial sludge volume of 3.5 L, deionized water was added with enriched sludge in the following runs as well. Nitrogen gas was sparged in the LBR before the start of the experiments to create anaerobic conditions. Leachate was continuously mixed in the LBR using a peristaltic pump (Masterflex L/S Economy Drive, Model no. 07554-90, USA) at a rate of 60 L/h. Another peristaltic pump (Masterflex L/S Digital Drive, 600 RPM, 115/230 VAC, Model no. 07523-80, USA) was programmed to regularly spray the leachate on FW at a rate of 88 L/h for 15, 30 and 45s at every 5 min, which created leachate recirculation rates from 4.4 to 13.2 L/h in the LBR. During the experiment the reaction time was fixed at 7 days.

The programmable pump was used for AnMBR feed and permeate. We programmed the permeate pump with permeation production for 4min followed by 1 min relaxation. Two types of UF membrane modules (module 1 and module 2) were used to maintain 0.45 and 0.84 LMH (with membrane module 1, phases 1 and 2) and 6 LMH (with membrane module 2, phase 3) (see supplementary material). A pressure transducer (Model: 68075-32, Cole Parmer, Canada) was connected to a data acquisition system (USB 6341, National Instruments, USA). The pressure drop and TMP were monitored using the pressure transducer and Labview Signal Express.. To mitigate cake formation on membranes, a shear flow was created by circulating the biogas and bulk liquid at rates of 13 L/min and 0.45 L/min with a peristaltic pump (Model 07554-90, Cole-Parmer, USA) and an air pump (KNF N811 KVP vacuum/pressure pump, Cole-Parmer, USA), respectively.

Chemical cleaning of the membranes was performed when the membrane flux decreased more than 20% or TMP increased over 40 kPa. The membrane-cleaning procedure consisted of four cycles. Each cycle consisted backflushing of 2000 mg/L citric acid, followed by deionized water, 200 mg/L NaOCl and deionized water. Cleaning chemicals including water were pumped through the membranes intermittently inside the reactor for 40 s in every 3 min at 14.5 psig in each cycle.


2.3 Characteristics of FW, LBR leachate and inocula

The FW was collected once every three months from a cafeteria at the University of Waterloo (Waterloo, ON, Canada). The FW characteristics were TCOD concentration = 265 ± 104.74 g/kg, TS concentration = 205.5 ± 82 g/kg, VS concentration = 194.3 ± 80 g/kg, VS/TS ratio = 94 ± 1.8 and specific COD = 1.38 ± 0.5 COD/VS. To inoculate the LBR, AD sludge (pH = 7.11 ± 0.2, TCOD concentration = 22.3 ± 1.9 g/L, SCOD concentration = 0.29 ± 0.01 g/L, TSS concentration = 24.85 ± 0.21 g/L and VSS concentration = 15.5 ± 0.5 g/L) was sampled from Kitchener wastewater treatment facility (Ontario, Canada) and used for the experiments. After collection, both FW and AD sludge were pretreated, stored and used in the experiments as suggested by Xiong et al. (2019b). The pH, TCOD, SCOD, TSS and VSS concentrations of the AD sludge were 7.16 ± 0.3, 26.72 ± 0.02 g/L, 3.15 ± 0.27 g/L, 14.15 ± 0.9 g/L and 12.33 ± 1.1 g/L respectively. After each LBR run, FW leachate was centrifuged at 9500 rpm for 20 min and recycled back to the LBR to enrich FW fermenters for internal inoculation to the LBR, as well as reduction of solid loading to the AnMBR. The microorganisms in the AnMBR were enriched by batch operation for two months approximately.


2.4 Analytical methods

Important water quality parameters including solid concentration (total solids (TS), volatile solids (VS), total suspended solids (TSS), total volatile solids (VSS)) and organic concentration (total chemical oxygen demand (TCOD) and soluble chemical oxygen demand (SCOD)were analyzed according to the standard methods.. Volatile fatty acids were analyzed with a gas chromatography (HP 5890 Series II, Hewlett Packard, USA) equipped with a flame ionization detector (GC-FID). Detailed method for VFA measurement is described elsewhere (Xiong et al., 2019b). Composition of biogas from the AnMBR and LBR was analyzed using a GC (model 310, SRI Instrument, 51 USA) equipped with a thermal conductivity detector (GC-TCD). Details of the method for gas analysis is described elsewhere (Galib et al., 2016).

HACH vials (TNTplus™ 880 for TKN, TNTplus™ 832 for NH_4 ^+-N and TNTplus™ 844 for TP and RP) were used for the estimation of nutrient concentrations such as total Kjeldahl nitrogen (TKN), ammonia nitrogen (NH_4 ^+-N), total phosphate (TP) and reactive phosphate (RP) LBR leachate was filtered with 0.45 μm syringe filters to determine the solubilization of solid nutrients. All chemical analyses were performed in triplicates.

2.6 Microbial community and statistical analysis

Leachate samples (40 mL) and food residues were sampled for analysis of microbial community in the LBR. In the AnMBR, 40 mL of mixed liquor was collected from the liquid recirculation line for microbial community analysis. Genomic DNA extraction and 16S rRNA sequencing was performed as described in the literature (Xiong et al., 2019b). Genomic DNA extraction of cells in both, LBR Leachate and mixed liquor from AnMBR, was performed using Sox DNA Isolation based on the recommendation of Metagenom Bio Inc. Then, PCR was performed with 25 μl of PCR reaction mixture: 5.0 μl of genomic DNA, 2.5 μl of 10x Taq buffer, 0.2 μl of Taq DNA polymerase (5u/μl), 0.5 μl of 10 mM dNTP, 5.0 μl of 1 μM forward primer and 5.0 μl of 1 μM reverse primer. 6.55 μl of PCR water was filled to reach 25 μl sample in total. Pro341F (CCTACGGGNBGCASCAG) and Pro805R(GACTACNVGGGTATCTAATCC) was used as the forward and reverse primer, respectively for V3–V4 region of 16S rRNA genes amplification. Each sample was prepared as triplicates. The PCR reaction includes initial DNA denaturation (95 °C for 5 min), 35 cycles of DNA denaturation (95 °C for 30 s), annealing (30 °C for 30 s) and extension (72 °C for 50 s), and then final extension (72 °C for 10 min). DNA in the amplicons were measured by Qubit dsDNA HS assay Kit (thermos Fisher Scientific Inc.) and then were pooled with same DNA amounts. MiSeq sequencer (Illumina Inc.) was used to sequence 2 × 250 bp of DNAs. A taxonomic analysis was performed by QIIME2 (v.2018.2), including removal of forward and reverse PCR primers, truncation of the forward and reverse reads (245bp), and then merging paired reads. Chimeric sequences were removed, then representative sequences were retrieved after clustering at 3% similarity. Using a naïve Bayesian classifier in QIIME2, the taxonomy of representative sequences was identified with SILVA 132 DB at 99% identity.

Analysis of variance (ANOVA) test using General Linear Model (GLM) method was performed to investigate leachate circulation rate effects (P ≤ 0.05) using R programming. Means were compared using Duncan's comparison tests (P ≤ 0.05).


3 Results and discussion


3.1 Solubilization and acidification of FW and solid nutrients in the LBR

In the LBR, FW was hydrolyzed and acidified leading to the formation of different soluble products such as volatile fatty acids and alcohols (Saha and Lee, 2020). As hydrolysis is typically the rate-limiting step in FW fermentation, leachate circulation rate is an important parameter to ensure enough microbial contact with the solid FW (Hussain et al., 2017). In comparison, literature already optimized pH and reaction time for FW stabilization in LBRs (Hussain et al., 2021; Shewa et al., 2020; Xiong et al., 2019a, 2019b). Higher leachate recirculation rates (13.3–8.8 L/h) resulted in higher hydrolysis and acidification yields (P < 0.05) (Table 1 ). For example, the hydrolysis yield of 538 g SCOD/kg VS_added at a recirculation rate of 13.2 L/h was 18% higher (456 g SCOD/kg VS_added) than that obtained at 4.4 L/h. Similarly, the acidification yield (468 g SCOD/kg VS_added) at 13.2 L/h was 29% higher than that obtained at 4.4 L/h (Table 1). Leachate recirculation rates higher than 13.2 L/h may result in higher hydrolysis and acidogenic yields within the fermentation time of 7 days (i.e., hydrolysis and acidogenic rates). However, higher recirculation rates would require careful modification of the LBR design to prevent clogging of the food waste bed and accumulation of the leachate. Future studies can investigate the addition of microporous materials such as such as granular activated carbon (GAC) in the food waste bed to maintain leachate permeability at higher leachate recirculation rates (Radadiya et al., 2022). Other design modifications could include the presence of perforated tubes in the food waste holding bed to allow leachate passage at high recirculation rates. Post-treatment of aerobic bioprocess would be needed to meet the final effluent standards including surcharge program rules, which depends on local needs and demands. In this case, LBR-AD followed by aerobic MBR could be considered.

Similar trend was also obtained for the solubilization of particulate nutrient data. Release of nutrients such as NH_4 ^+-N (1026-1,690 mg/L) and phosphate (391-1302 mg/L) from FW in leachate significantly increased at higher leachate recirculation rates (8.8–13.2 L/h) (Table 1). This suggested that higher leachate circulation rate increased degradation of proteins and phosphorus-rich FW. Leachate circulation rate, on the other hand, did not influence fermentation pathways significantly in the LBR when relatively consistent end product distribution was considered. Butyric acid was the largest VFA comprising 37–44.6% of the COD, followed by acetic acid and propionic acid in all cases.


3.2 Permeate quality and biomass concentration in the AnMBR

The centrifuged leachate had a high COD concentration (24.42 ± 4.82 g/L) and was further stabilized using the AnMBR in this study. As shown in Fig. 2 (a), the average permeate COD concentrations in three phases were relatively low. No suspended solids were detected in the effluent of the AnMBR, indicating complete removal of particulate matters through ultrafiltration membrane separation. The lowest permeate COD concentration was found to be 1.78 ± 1.15 g/L at HRT 25 d, SRT 130 d and OLR 0.75 ± 0.11 kg COD/m^3/d. Increase in permeate COD concentration (4.46 ± 0.34 g/L) was observed when HRT was decreased from 25 d to 13 d in Phase 2. This suggests incomplete acetogenesis and methanogenesis in 13 d of short reaction time, as compared to HRT 25 d. Similar COD removal efficiencies in Phase 2 and 3 (84.3 ± 2.4% and 86.4 ± 3.0%) indicated that SRT change from 130 d to 75 d in Phase 3 did not influence COD removal.

Consistent to COD, total VFA concentration was also the lowest in permeate at Phase 1 (1.52 ± 1.14 g COD/L), as illustrated in Fig. 2(b). Acetic acid (0.82 ± 0.39 g COD/L) was found the dominant VFA in this case. However, with higher OLR in Phase 2 and 3, the total VFA concentration increased to 4.04 ± 0.59 g COD/L and dominant VFA started to shift towards propionic acid from acetic acid. This result suggested that for Phase 2 and 3 (short HRT 13 days) acetogenesis might be limited in the AnMBR. Thus, ensuring higher composition of acetic acid in the LBR leachate could result in higher COD removal efficiency and shorter HRTs in the AnMBR. Future studies can optimize H_2 partial pressure in the LBR, pH and inoculum (Braga Nan et al., 2020; Li et al., 2019; Sivalingam et al., 2021) to obtain high composition of acetic acid in the LBR leachate.

Average biomass concentration in the reactor was 1.99 ± 0.53 g MLVSS/L at in Phase 1 (HRT of 25d and OLR of 0.75 ± 0.11 kg COD/m^3/d). Decrease in HRT and increase in OLR led to increase of biomass concentration to 4.27 ± 0.26 g MLVSS/L in Phase 2. Biomass concentration decreased to 3.15 ± 0.41 g MLVSS/L in Phase 3 as compared to Phase 2 when more sludge from the AnMBR was withdrawn to meet an effective volume of 5 L.

Nutrient concentrations (NH_4 ^+-N, total phosphate) in permeate were found very close to the feed concentrations (LBR leachate), implying very small nutrient removal in the AnMBR (see supplementary material). The concentrations of NH_4 ^+-N, total phosphate and reactive phosphate were 317–403, 23–213, and 20–211 mg/L, respectively. Such high concentration of nutrients without particulates and pathogens can be an ideal liquid fertilizer potentially mixed with animal manure. AnMBR permeate can be used for hydroponic nutrients (Pelayo Lind et al., 2021; Weimers et al., 2022; Wilkie, 2014), but it needs further investigation to assess the impacts of residual VFA (⁓4 g/L) on target plants.


3.3 Biogas production

Biogas from the LBR comprised mainly of H_2 and CO_2 without CH_4. Interestingly, hydrogen production was ceased after 2 d in all tests, which implies that fermentative biohydrogen would be completed for 2 d. Leachate circulation rate played a vital role in hydrogen evolution rate in the LBR. Increase of leachate circulation rate from 4.4 L/h to 8.8 L/h improved hydrogen production volume from 0.76 L to 3.2 L. This means 4 times more production of hydrogen gas when leachate circulation rate was doubled. Slow mixing conditions kept in the LBR might limit mass transport of hydrogen molecules from the leachate to the headspace. However, increment of leachate circulation rate from 8.8 L/h to 13.2 L/h did not improve biohydrogen production much (from 3.2 L to 3.27 L) probably due to clogging issues, which can limit the mass transfer of hydrogen. In this study, the maximum hydrogen yield in the LBR was found at 21.15 L H_2/kg VS_added, which is higher than other studies involving FW as feedstock (Shen et al., 2013; Voelklein et al., 2016). This may be due to the presence of higher quantity of readily fermentable FW.

In the AnMBR, methane production rate increased with increasing OLR (Fig. 2(C)). In three phases, methane generation was 0.8 ± 0.12, 2.86 ± 0.52 and 2.95 ± 0.43 L/d, respectively, accounting for 72 ± 5% of the biogas. Normalized with the mass of COD removed, average specific methane yields were found higher in Phases 2 and 3 than Phase 1 (0.24 ± 0.05, 0.30 ± 0.05 and 0.31 ± 0.06 L/g COD_removed in three phases) suggesting higher degradability of FW in latter phases. Maximum specific methane yield in this study was observed to be 0.31 ± 0.06 L/g COD_removed, which is 82% of the theoretical specific methane yield value (0.38 L/g COD at 22 °C). This result proves that the LBR pre-treatment provides ideal substrate to the subsequent AnMBR for methane yield. The performance of the AnMBR was comparable to those reported for AD systems operated at mesophilic (35–38 °C) and thermophilic temperatures (50 °C) at similar organic loading rates and substrates (Table 2 ) The advantage of AnMBR is even more profound when compared to other AD systems operated at room temperatures which required very long HRT (>100 days) to achieve reasonable methane yields on food waste (Table 2).

The methane production rate and the specific methane yield was relatively constant at SRT change (SRT 130 d–75 d) in the AnMBR, suggesting that endogenous decay did not contribute to methane production in the AnMBR. Including H_2 and CH_4 gases, the two-stage system produced 9579 L of H_2 (34 kWh) and 73,983 L of CH_4 (816 kWh) per ton of FW treated, which is equivalent to 850 kWh per ton of FW treated (assuming higher heating value of H_2 and CH_4 are 12.68 MJ/m^3 H_2 and 39.71 MJ/m^3 CH_4 respectively) (Ali and Basit, 1993; Xia et al., 2015). This energy yield comparable to literature values operated at higher temperatures and longer reactions times (Table 3 ).


3.4 Membrane fouling and maintenance cleaning for the AnMBR

The average permeate fluxes in this study were 0.45 ± 0.01, 0.84 ± 0.01 and 5.99 ± 0.58 LMH in three phases (Fig. 3 ). No significant fouling was observed during the operation period of Phase 1 when high shear flow of biogas was mainted in the AnMBR run at a low permeation rate,. As a result, maintenance cleaning was not required. TMP was observed to be 4.9 ± 1.62 kPa in this phase with an average increase (dTMP/dt) of 0.27 kPa/d in this phase. In Phase 2 (HRT 13 d), the average flux almost doubled, but permeate flux was decreased from 0.87 to 0.67 LMH in the first 28 days of operation in Phase 2, indicating membrane fouling. However, the flux was recovered by the maintenance cleaning on the 29th day. It was noticeable that dTMP/dt increased from 0.44 kPa/day to 1.07 kPa/day after the first cleaning in Phase 2. This suggested that maintenance cleaning would not completely eliminate foulants from the membranes.

We changed membrane 1 with membrane 2 in Phase 3 to increase membrane flux at a constant HRT of 13 d; this membrane change allowed us to evaluate membrane fouling in the constant OLR in the AnMBR. Membrane fouling frequently occurred and higher dTMP/dt value of 6.35 kPa/d was measured, which suggests more severe fouling trend. Hence, we could not keep SRT at 130 d, and then reduced it to 75 d. Regular maintenance cleaning at every five days was essential to keep an average permeate flux around 6 LMH including TMP less than 40 kPa recommended by the membrane manufacturer.


3.5 Microbial community in LBR and AnMBR

Fig. 4 showed microbial communities between the LBR and AnMBR. In the LBR, Roseburia, Enterococcus, Clostridium and Bacteroides were identified from both fermented FW and leachate. Roseburia and Bacteroides were also found in an LBR for FW fermentation (Xiong et al., 2019b), and they are well known for VFA-producing bacteria by fermenting dietary fibers into butyrate and acetate, respectively (Mirande et al., 2010; Thomas et al., 2011). Clostridium is one of the major acidogens which also produce acetate and butyrate through fermentation of dietary fibers (Cabrol et al., 2017; Chinda et al., 2004). This genus is also well known for biohydrogen production (Dong et al., 2009). Enterococcus is also identified in biohydrogen production of FW fermentation (Yin and Wang, 2019). Microbial communities between leachate and fermented FW showed similar patterns although relative abundances of those genera were slightly different. This is consistent to the literature (Xiong et al., 2019b).

Microbial communities in the AnMBR were totally different from the LBR, as expected. Methanogenic archaea such as Methanocorpusculum (Zellner et al., 1987) and Methanosaeta (Ma et al., 2006) were present in both HRT 25d and 13d. Methanocorpusculum do not require high temperature for their growth, which suggests that this genus could play an important role on methane production in the AnMBR operated at room temperature conditions. Rikenellaceae family was identified in both HRT conditions, but its population increased at HRT 13d. This family is possibly related to carbohydrate-fermentation biohydrogen and VFAs production (Su et al., 2014), which is consistent to higher VFAs in HRT 13d (Fig. 2). Syntrophomonas, highly dominant genus in both HRT conditions, have also been found in other AnMBR studies (Smith et al., 2015). It is well known for degrading butyrate followed by producing acetate and H_2 through syntrophic interaction with methanogens (Zhang et al., 2004).


3.6 COD balance and AnMBR permeate

The COD balance was built for the two-stage process using the average TCOD of initial FW (265 ± 104.74 g/L). Similar trends were found in all combined conditions of the LBR and AnMBR. Hence only COD balance for LBR_4.4 and AnMBR with HRT 13 d and SRT 75 d is discussed here. The largest electron sinks were residual FW in the LBR and methane in the AnMBR, accounting for 36.7% and 32.1% of the input COD, respectively (see supplementary material). Cell growth in the two bioreactors cumulatively contributed to 5.48% of input COD. Biohydrogen only accounted for 0.02% of input COD. AnMBR permeate contributed to 7.37% of initial FW COD, and centrifugation of the leachate returned to the LBR accounted for ∼3% of initial FW COD. There was unaccounted COD of 15.4% (unidentified fraction of COD), which may be due to the presence of dissolved methane in membrane permeate, COD consumption coupled to sulfate reduction, biomass attached on reactor wall or membrane surface in the AnMBR. This unaccounted COD fraction is often identified in bio-hydrogen or anaerobic digestion works especially using real feedstock, like our study (Blanco et al., 2019; Gulhane et al., 2016).

Remaining FW COD in the LBR was as high as 37% of input FW, which means that post-treatment of residual FW is important for commercialization of the combined process. Considering high biodegradability of residual FW and partial fermentation, reuse of the FW as organic fertilizer can improve cost efficacy and sustainability of the integrated bioprocess in the context of a circular bioeconomy. More study is required to characterize residual FW for organic fertilizer application.


4 Conclusion

This study evaluated the performance of a two-stage process (LBR + AnMBR) for FW treatment and energy recovery. High leachate circulation rate of 13.2 L/h enhanced FW hydrolysis and acidification by 17–29% in comparison to low recirculation rate of 4.4 L/h. Majority of VFAs comprised of butyric, acetic and propionic acid. The AnMBR further clarified residual COD after LBR fermentation, and COD removal and methane production as high as 86% and ⁓ 3 L/d was achieved at a low HRT of just 13 days. Maintenance cleaning was needed at least once every five days to keep a membrane flux of 6 LMH in the AnMBR. The combined bioprocess improved bioenergy production from FW, creating a bioenergy of 850 kWh per ton FW_treated, along with nutrients-rich, particulate-free AnMBR permeates that can be applied for landscape use or liquid fertilizer.
