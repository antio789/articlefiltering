TITLE: Anaerobic digestion of sulfate-acidified cattle slurry: One-stage vs. two-stage


1 Introduction

Slurry acidification with sulfuric acid (H_2SO_4) is used in Denmark at farm level to decrease ammonia (NH_3) emission (Kai et al., 2008; Dai and Blanes-Vidal, 2013). Important reductions in carbon dioxide (CO_2), methane (CH_4) and hydrogen sulfide (H_2S) emissions have also been observed during storage of acidified slurries with H_2SO_4 (Petersen et al., 2012; Dai and Blanes-Vidal, 2013). Acidification with H_2SO_4 is considered an appropriate technology to conserve initial slurry composition and to avoid microbial activity during storage (Ottosen et al., 2009); the reasons, however, are less evident.

There are different possible explanations for the observed cessation of microbial activity in H_2SO_4-acidified slurries, but it is primarily due to the sudden decrease in slurry pH and the toxicity of the subsequent organic acids and sulfide (H_2S) generated (Chen et al., 2008). However, in an anaerobic digestion of animal slurry with a low H_2SO_4 concentration and no apparent change in the pH of the effluent, a significant reduction was nevertheless observed in microbial activity (Moset et al., 2012a, b). The explanation for the absence of microbial activity in those cases could be because of the creation of an imbalance by the H_2S produced from H_2SO_4 to the symbiotic relationships between different microbial groups (fermentative microorganisms, acidogens, acetogens, sulfate-reducing bacteria (SRB) and methanogens) that are formed to completely degrade the organic matter (Hulshoff Pol et al., 1998).

Surprisingly, these previous works carried out by Moset et al. (2012a, b) also showed that low inclusion levels of H_2SO_4-acidified slurries can increase CH_4 production in anaerobic reactors. Therefore, more research is needed to understand the microbial dynamics in anaerobic digestion of H_2SO_4-acidified slurries and to suggest technology that can improve the synergism between acidification and anaerobic digestion of animal manure.

In the present work we hypothesize that a two-stage thermophilic anaerobic digestion process could be an appropriate technology for acidified cattle manure; not only for the separation of SRB from methanogens, since as Vallero et al. (2004) showed, SRB can outcompete methanogens when the temperature increases from 55 to 65 °C in a methanol-fed sulfidogenic UASB operated at 55 °C, but also because a first thermophilic step has been shown to be an appropriate technology for lignocellulosic substrates in a two-step anaerobic digestion process (Gavala et al., 2003, Orozco et al., 2013).

The desired two-step anaerobic digestion mechanism designed in this work consists of a first step at 65 °C (low thermal hydrolysis), followed by a second step at the normal thermophilic conditions of 50 °C. Biological sulfur removal (sulfate reduction and H_2S volatilization) and hydrolysis will potentially be achieved in the first step. A reduction in the methanogenesis inhibition by H_2S or even a methanogenesis enhancement could then be expected in the second step through increased availability of substrates produced in step 1 (hydrolysis). A comparable technology has been successfully tested in sulfur-rich tannery wastewater (Genschow et al., 1996) and acrylic fiber manufacturing wastewater (Li et al., 2012) where simple organic substrates are used. To our knowledge, the application of the concept in complex and slowly biodegradable substrates like cattle manure has not been investigated.

Therefore, the aims of the present work were, firstly, to determine the inhibitory thresholds for anaerobic digestion of acidified cattle manure in a long-term operation and, secondly, to evaluate the efficiency of a two-stage anaerobic digestion process in terms of biological sulfur removal and CH_4 yield with different inclusion levels of acidified slurry. To this end, two different levels of acidified cattle manure (10% and 20%) were tested in CSTRs in a long-term study.


2 Materials and methods


2.1 Substrates and anaerobic digesters

The two cattle manures (CM) used in this experiment were obtained at the experimental farms at Research Centre Foulum (Aarhus University, Denmark). Table 1 shows the composition of the influents used to feed the reactors in each period. In this experiment, acidified cattle manure (AcCM) was produced according to the procedure followed in farm conditions, whereby H_2SO_4 is added during continuous agitation in order to avoid foam and the conversion of sulfate ion into H_2S. The amount of H_2SO_4 added was controlled by a pH-sensor, setting a maximum pH level of 5.5. The exact SO_4 concentration in the AcCM used in each experimental period was analyzed (Table 1). As expected, the AcCM had a pH below 5.5 and a SO_4 concentration of more than 10 g L^−1 in the periods when it was used to feed the reactors (periods 2 and 3). These pH and SO_4 concentrations are in the range of those found in the bibliography using acidified slurry from commercial farms (Moset et al., 2012b).

Inoculum for starting up the reactors was collected from the anaerobic reactors located at Research Centre Foulum (Aarhus, Denmark) that process cattle manure at thermophilic conditions.

Four CSTRs were used in this work: three pilot reactors (PR), two with working capacities of 100 L and one with 80 L; and one laboratory reactor (R65) with a 20 L working capacity (Table 2 and Fig. 1 ). The three PRs used are of similar design, using water-filled heating tubes inside the reactor to control the temperature. The body of the reactor carries an insulating jacket to ensure a stable operating temperature. In the R65 reactor, the temperature is controlled by electrical resistances located at the bottom of the reactor. In all reactors, continuous mixing was performed by a central shaft with a propeller at the bottom rotating at 140 rpm.

In order to measure the volume of the biogas produced, PR reactors were continuously connected to a gas collection cylinder placed in an oil solution. In the R65 reactor, gas production was measured using an automatic CH_4 potential system (AMPTS II, Bioprocess Control, Beijing, China).


2.2 Experimental procedure

The operational parameters of the reactors are shown in Table 2 and Fig. 1. Two of the PR reactors (PR1 and PR2) were run as single-stage digesters; the other (PR3) was run as a two-stage digester in combination with the R65. PR1 and PR2 worked at a thermophilic temperature (50 °C) with a hydraulic retention time (HRT) of 20 days. In the two-stage anaerobic digestion process, R65 was used for the initial low thermal hydrolysis at 65 °C with a HRT of 4 days and PR3 was used for the methanogenesis at 50 °C with an HRT of 16 days.

Total HRT for the three treatments was 20 days. The total duration of the experiment was 120 days. Three periods were designated based on feeding composition:• Period 1 (from day 1 to day 28). During this period the four reactors were working with 100% cattle manure to reach stability. • Period 2 (from day 29 to day 77). In this period, PR2 and R65 processed a mixture of 10% AcCM +90% CM. PR3 was fed the effluent from R65. • Period 3 (from day 78 to day 120). In this period PR2 and R65 were fed a mixture of 20% AcCM +80% CM. PR3 was fed the effluent from R65.

During the whole experimental period (from day 1 to day 120), PR1 was used as a control, running on 100% normal cattle manure.


2.3 Chemical analyses

Samples from each reactor and from the influent were taken weekly and analyzed for: total solids (TS) and volatile solids (VS), according to APHA (2005); total ammonia (TAN), using photometric kits (kit number 1.14555.0001, Spectroquant^® kit, Merck, USA); VFA, using a gas chromatograph (5560-D of APHA, 2005) equipped with a flame ionization detector (HP 68050 series, Heweltt Packard); pH, measured from fresh samples using a pH-meter (Portamess^® 911 pH, Knick, Germany), and sulfate (SO_4 ^2+), using photometric kits (kit number 1.00617.0001, Spectroquant^® kit, Merck, USA) in filtrated fresh samples.

Biogas samples were taken twice a week in each reactor by flushing a 22-mL sample bottle with 300 mL of biogas to determine CH_4, CO_2 and H_2S concentration in the biogas using a Perkin Elmer Clarus 500 gas chromatograph equipped with a thermal conductivity detector.


2.4 Calculations

In each experimental period, average digestate composition and CH_4 yield were determined after 1 HRT. At the end of period 1 (day 60), the reactors were not fed due to winter holidays. The reactor parameters derived from this period were not included in the calculations.

Corrected VS (VS_C) were calculated by partially adding VFA to VS in order to estimate volatile compounds lost during drying. Volatile fatty acids were added to VS in the following manner: 100% of VFA was added to VS in manures with a pH below 7, 80% of VFA was added to VS in manures with a pH between 7 and 8, 10% of VFA was added to VS in manures with a pH higher than 8 (Moset et al., 2012b).

The sulfur balance in this work was calculated by using the SO_4 reduction and H_2S concentration in the biogas. 3 Results


3.1 Anaerobic digestion process in PR1 (control)

The control reactor (PR1) showed stable performances in terms of solids concentration, solids removal and pH during the experimental period (Tables 3 and 4 ). Small differences between periods 2 and 3 were found in the average TAN concentration and SO_4 ^2−, these parameters being higher in period 3 than in period 2 (Tables 3 and 4, respectively). However, the SO_4 ^2− reduced from the influent stream (Table 4), and the H_2S concentration in the biogas (Fig. 2 ) were very low in both periods (<10% and <3500 ppm, respectively).

The main difference in PR1 performance between periods 2 and 3 was in VFA (Fig. 2). In period 2, the VFA concentration in PR1 was lower than 0.55 g L^−1, especially between days 38 and 58, with acetic acid at 82% being the most dominant VFA. The average (±standard deviation) CH_4 yield in the last 20 days of this period was 185.9 ± 15.50 L CH_4 kgVS^−1. In period 3, not only total VFA, but also other individual VFAs besides acetic acid had accumulated in PR1. The higher VFA accumulation in PR1 was observed on day 92, where the total VFA concentration in the reactor was higher than 3 g L^−1, less than 45% of which was acetic acid. This accumulation of VFAs in reactors coincided with a decrease in CH_4 yield in PR1 (Fig. 3 ) from 219 L CH_4 kgVS^−1 on day 70–133 L CH_4 kgVS^−1 on day 88. After this period, a recovery in CH_4 yield was observed in PR1, with an average (±standard deviation) CH_4 yield for the last 20 days of period 3 of 165.9 ± 15.81 L CH_4 kgVS^−1. The recovery of the CH_4 yield in PR1 was achieved from day 69, whereas the recovery of the VFA concentration was achieved later, from day 109.


3.2 One-step anaerobic co-digestion of acidified and raw cattle manure in PR2

A slightly higher solids concentration and lower solids reduction were observed in PR2 compared with PR1 in period 2 (Table 3), but the differences were smaller in period 3. In fact, when comparing the effluent composition in PR2 between periods, a lower solids concentration in the effluent (and thus higher solids reduction) was observed in period 3 compared with period 2 (Table 3).

A higher SO_4 ^2− reduction was obtained in PR2 than in PR1, where it again was higher in period 3 than in period 2. A low and stable H_2S concentration in the biogas was observed until the middle of period 2 (day 54) in PR2 (Fig. 2) where after it increased to approx. 15,000 ppm. In this period an average of 0.02 g S-H_2S L^−1 slurry was removed from the liquid (Table 3), which means that only 3% of the SO_4 ^2− in the influent and around 20% of the SO_4 ^2− reduced in the reactors was stripped with the biogas in PR2 during period 2. In period 3, the H_2S concentration in the biogas and thus the H_2S stripped increased. On average for this period, 0.35 gS-H_2S L^−1 slurry was removed from the liquid −25% of the SO_4 ^2− introduced in the reactor and 75% of the SO_4 ^2− reduced.

The total VFA concentration in PR2 was similar to that observed in PR1 during the first 58 days (1 g L^−1, Fig. 2), with acetic acid accounting for more than 80%. In period 3, however, differences between PR1 and PR2 in VFA evolution were evident. A progressive accumulation of VFA was observed in PR2 during period 3 to a total VFA concentration at the end of period 3 of around 5 g L^−1, consisting mainly of acetic (about 64%), propionic and butyric acids (Fig. 1), although the pH remained constant and slightly lower than 8 during this period.

The average (±standard deviation) CH_4 yield in PR2 in the last 20 days of period 2 was 203.8 ± 12.70 L CH_4 kgVS^−1. According to Eq. (2), this is a 10% improvement in CH_4 yield in PR2 over PR1 when 10% AcCM is used in co-digestion with 90% CM in PR2, compared to the single-substrate anaerobic digestion of CM in PR1. In period 3, however, reductions in CH_4 yield were observed in PR2, especially from day 97. The average (±standard deviation) CH_4 yield in the last 20 days of period 3 was 102.7 ± 24.40 L CH_4 kgVS^−1, meaning a CH_4 yield reduction of around 38%.


3.3 Efficiency of the low thermal hydrolysis (first step)

Total and volatile solids concentrations decreased by nearly 12% during the first step of anaerobic digestion at 65 °C in both periods (Table 3). However, the VS_C reduction was lower than 10%, probably because of the high VFA concentration in R65. In fact, the VFA concentration increased by around 50% after the first step during period 2 and by around 43% during period 3 (Fig. 2). The ratio of acetic acid/total VFA increased by 11% in period 2 and by 4% in period 3. Furthermore, the ratio of total VFA/VS_C increased by around 60% in R65 in both periods.

Biogas production in R65 during both periods was lower than 5 L kgVS^−1 with a CH_4 concentration of less than 20% (data not shown). Biological sulfur removal occurred in R65 during both periods (31% and 25% of SO_4 ^2−, respectively) (Table 4) and the H_2S concentration in the biogas produced in R65 increased from the beginning of period 2 until the beginning of period 3 (Fig. 2) where it apparently stabilized at 25,000–30,000 ppm.


3.4 Anaerobic digestion process in PR3 (second step)

A higher solids reduction was obtained in PR3 than in PR2 in period 2 (Table 3). Taking R65 and PR3 together, TS, VS and VSc reductions were 19.0, 32.4 and 29.9%, respectively, in period 2.

Although the SO_4 ^2− reduction in PR3 was very small, the SO_4 ^2− concentration in PR3 was lower than in PR2 in both periods, but particularly so in period 2. In addition, a higher biogas H_2S concentration was found in PR3 than in PR2, especially at the beginning of period 3.

The VFA concentration in PR3 during period 2 was lower than 0.35 g L^−1 and was particularly low from day 31–58, consisting almost entirely of acetic acid. However, significant accumulations of VFA were observed in PR3 during period 3, especially from day 109 to day 112 where the VFA concentration in PR3 was higher than 10 g L^−1. After this day, a significant reduction in VFA concentration in PR3 was observed (Fig. 2), especially in acetic acid.

As shown in Fig. 3, the CH_4 yield in PR3 fell significantly from day 79–110 and was in fact almost zero from day 95–110. From day 110, however, the CH_4 in PR3 rose strongly. The average daily (±standard deviation) CH_4 yield in the last 9 days of period 3 was 157.7 ± 22.52 L CH_4 kgVS^−1, i.e. a CH_4 yield inhibition of less than 5% (Eq. (2)).


4 Discussion

Co-digestion of 10% of H_2SO_4-acidified slurry with 90% non-acidified cattle manure showed a stable anaerobic digestion process in PR2 and in R65 + PR3, with an increase in CH_4 yield in PR2 of around 10% compared with the control reactor (PR1). In this period the SO_4 ^2− concentration was reduced in the reactors. Moset et al. (2012b) also observed a boost in CH_4 yield (nearly 20%) when 10% acidified pig manure was co-digested with raw pig manure. There may be different reasons for the elevated CH_4 yields at low inclusion levels of H_2SO_4-acidified slurry in anaerobic reactors, such as hydrolysis enhancement (Ramos, 2003); the precipitation of toxic heavy metals like Cr, Cu, Zn (Chen et al., 2008) when SO_4 ^2− is reduced to H_2S (Colleran et al., 1995, Muyzer and Stams, 2008) or the uptake of sulfur as a nutrient by bacteria (Briones et al., 2009; O'Flaherty et al., 1998). Although the results obtained in this study did not directly reveal the origin of this improvement, they confirm that low levels of H_2SO_4-acidified manure are beneficial for anaerobic digesters. In addition, these results clearly indicate that when adding low levels of acidified slurry, methanogens and SRB can live in equilibrium without inhibiting CH_4 production.

In the two-step anaerobic digestion process, an apparent separation into bacterial processes was achieved when 10% acidified SO_4 slurry was used. In the R65 reactor with a very low CH_4 production, there was an accumulation of VFAs and a strong SO_4 ^2− reduction. This means that the microbial groups responsible for the first steps of the anaerobic degradation of organic matter (hydrolysis and acidogenesis), but not methanogens, were established in R65. The second step (PR3) was dominated by methanogenesis, as reflected in the reductions in VFA concentrations, in the high CH_4 production and low SO_4 ^2− reduction. These results are consistent with those of Gavala et al. (2003) who found that methanogens, but not hydrolytic bacteria, are inhibited at temperatures between 55 and 70 °C.

When looking at the entire process (R65 + PR3), higher solids concentrations and SO_4 ^2− reductions were obtained in the two-stage anaerobic reactor than in the one-step reactor (PR2). However, in terms of CH_4 yield, the two-step process showed no clear increase compared with the control reactor (PR1). In addition, the incomplete SO_4 ^2− reduction and the accumulation of dissolved organic matter in form of VFA could indicate that SRB were not completely established in R65. According to Lens et al. (1998), the critical stage in the two-step biological sulfur removal in industrial SO_4 ^2−-rich wastewaters is the complete reduction of SO_4 ^2− in the first step.

Complete organic matter removal via anaerobic SO_4 ^2− reduction is a complicated process for different reasons. Firstly, not only is it extremely dependent on both the organic matter/SO_4 ^2− ratio (Lens et al., 1998) and the establishment of other hydrogenotrophic microorganisms (Muyzer and Stams, 2008), but H_2S also accumulates in the liquid. The problems associated with H_2S accumulation are not only related to their specific toxicity (Koster et al., 1986; McCartney and Oleszkiewicz, 1991; Reis et al., 1992; Lens et al., 1998; O'Flaherty et al., 1998), but also to the precipitation of some nutrients (Isa et al., 1986; Colleran et al., 1995). The stripping of H_2S is made complicated by its low volatility and its peculiar dispersion over the liquid called “bubble release” (Ni et al., 2001 and Blanes-Vidal et al., 2009). Correct H_2S stripping requires thorough mixing and a high biogas production, especially at high pH. Therefore, the H_2S produced in R65 remained in the liquid phase and was transferred to the second step (PR3), where it was partially stripped with the biogas produced.

In terms of CH_4 production, when 10% of AcCM is co-digested with 90% of CM, the two-step anaerobic co-digestion yielded less than the single step. The fact that the single-step method worked better than the two-step indicates that the association between SRB and methanogens could be beneficial for the anaerobic digestion process. Syntrophic relationships between acetogens and SRB and between hydrogenotrophic methanogens and SRB (Colleran et al., 1995) have been described in the literature, but the exact reason for the overall stimulation of CH_4 yield when digesting a 90/10 mixture of raw and acidified slurry in a single step remains unclear.

Important inhibitions were evident in both systems when 20% of acidified slurry was co-digested with raw cattle slurry, although in PR3 the CH_4 inhibition started at the end of period 2. Therefore, a mixture of 20% AcCM in anaerobic co-digestion with CM is not appropriate in thermophilic CSTRs. In addition, although in PR2 the SO_4 ^2− reduction was higher in period 3 than in period 2, VFA accumulated in PR2 only during period 3, meaning that the CH_4 reduction did not correspond to an increase in SRB activity. This could indicate an important correlation between methanogens and SRB activity or that these two populations are inhibited by comparable levels of H_2S. On this topic, Moset et al. (2012b) also found that the co-digestion of 30% acidified pig slurry with raw pig slurry caused a reduction of around 30% in CH_4 yield in thermophilic anaerobic reactors and VFA accumulations.

The apparent recovery of CH_4 yield and VFA concentration in PR3 at the end of period 3 could be due to microbial adaptation. However, no reliable conclusions can be reached from this work in this regard, due to the short period under this apparent stability.


5 Conclusions and future perspectives

Thermophilic CSTRs can successfully operate with 10% AcCM and 90% CM. In this study, a 10% higher CH_4 yield was observed along with important reductions of solids in the effluent. However, a higher percentage of AcCM (20%) in the mixture caused VFA accumulation in the reactor and a more than 30% reduction in CH_4 production. The reasons for this remain unclear, however, future studies should focus on microbial population changes in reactors with low H_2SO_4 concentrations.

During the first step of anaerobic digestion at 65 °C with 10% and 20% of AcCM in co-digestion with CM, hydrolysis, acidogenesis and partial sulfate reduction were achieved. However, a low efficiency of sulfide stripping from the liquid was achieved in the first step, and then a strong methanogenesis inhibition took place during the second step. Therefore, mechanisms to enhance sulfide losses between the first and the second step must be explored in order to advance this technology.
